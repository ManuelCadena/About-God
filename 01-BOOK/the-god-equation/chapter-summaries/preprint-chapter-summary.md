For instance, *Galileo Galilei* famously observed that "*Philosophy is written in this grand book -- I mean the universe... Such insights established the intuition that nature's orderliness and intelligibility point to a rational divine Mind. We survey Enlightenment and modern era testimonies: from **Johannes Kepler** and **Isaac Newton** (who saw scientific laws as reflecting divine arrangement) to 20th-century reflections like *Sir James Jeans'* remark that from the evidence of creation, "*the Great Architect of the Universe begins to appear as a pure mathematician*" . These historical voices underscore a persistent theme: the **mathematical harmony** of the cosmos has long been interpreted as a sign of an underlying intelligent cause. By providing this intellectual backdrop, the chapter shows that our book's informational-theological model echoes a rich tradition linking God with the **rational structure of reality**.

**Chapter 5: Cosmology and Existence -- Contingency, Origin, and Fine-Tuning**

Chapter 5 addresses evidence from **cosmology** and the mere existence of a universe. First, the **metaphysical contingency argument** is framed in analytic terms: the fact that anything (rather than nothing) exists at all suggests the need for a necessary, self-existent cause (as argued by Leibniz's question "Why is there something rather than nothing?"). We connect this to modern cosmology: the universe's beginning in the Big Bang implies a finite past and intensifies the question of an initial cause or condition. Second, we explore the remarkable **fine-tuning** of physical constants and initial conditions. The chapter cites the extraordinary precision in the Big Bang's initial state -- for instance, *Roger Penrose's* famous calculation that the odds of our low-entropy universe occurring by chance are on the order of 1 in 10\^10\^123 . Such an astronomically small probability is presented as evidence that the universe's life-permitting structure is not a random accident, but plausibly the result of intentional calibration (or else one must resort to a multiverse of extreme proportions). We review scientific literature on fine-tuning: e.g., the narrow ranges for forces and particle masses required for chemistry and stars, drawing on sources from physics (Martin Rees, *Sir John Polkinghorne*, etc.). Additionally, the chapter considers quantum-mechanical hints (the "QM" variable): ideas like *John Wheeler's* "participatory universe" and observer-dependent effects, which some interpret as the cosmos requiring a Mind. Wheeler's aphorism *"It from bit,"* suggesting that physical reality fundamentally arises from immaterial information , is discussed as a bridge between physics and our informational model (to be expanded later). We conclude the chapter by noting how both the contingency of existence and the fine-tuning of the cosmos make the **cosmological evidence** strongly favor theism within our probabilistic framework: a universe that began and is precisely ordered aligns more naturally with a creative **Mind or Designer** hypothesis than with chance alone . The logistic model's coefficient for cosmological evidence is thus justified as positive. Objections (like anthropic reasoning and multiverse theories) are addressed with academic responses and references.

**Chapter 6: Life and Mind -- Biological Complexity and Consciousness**

This chapter examines two deeply perplexing phenomena: the origin of **life** (biological complexity) and the emergence of **consciousness**. In the first part, we review the current scientific understanding (and gaps therein) regarding life's origin. Even with advanced biochemistry, the transition from non-living chemistry to a self-replicating cell remains **enigmatic**. We highlight the information-rich nature of life: the digital code in DNA and RNA, which stores instructions for building proteins, sits at the heart of a classic chicken-and-egg problem -- DNA encodes proteins, but proteins are required to form and replicate DNA . As one article puts it, *"The information encoded in DNA and RNA is required to make proteins; yet the proteins are required to make the nucleic acids... We have two chickens, two eggs, and no answer to which came first."* . This stands as a formidable puzzle for naturalistic scenarios. We survey estimates of the complexity and probability of forming even a simple genome by chance, referencing theorists like *Fred Hoyle* (who famously compared it to a tornado assembling a 747). While mainstream science has hypotheses (RNA world, metabolism-first, etc.), we cite current literature acknowledging the improbability or unknowns that remain . We then connect this to the **theistic interpretation**: that the specified complexity in biology (often described as "information content" in DNA/RNA) is *expected* if life was intentionally programmed by a divine mind . In the second part, we confront the "**Consciousness Gap**" -- the hard problem of explaining sentient, first-person consciousness in a purely material framework. We review arguments from philosophy of mind (e.g., *David Chalmers'* hard problem, *Thomas Nagel's* critique in *Mind and Cosmos*) and note that subjective experience so far eludes reduction to neural mechanisms. The chapter discusses why **mind** might be evidence for God: many theists argue that mind cannot arise from matter unless mind (i.e. a primordial conscious being) was "there" from the beginning. In our model, the consciousness variable gets a positive weight reflecting how a universe with conscious beings is far more probable if a super-mind (God) exists to produce other minds, versus if reality were fundamentally mindless. We provide thought experiments and references (such as *John Eccles* or *Karl Popper's* views on dualism) to support this. By the end, Chapter 6 has portrayed life and consciousness as **phenomena laden with information and purpose**, which naturalism struggles to fully account for. Their existence fits the hypothesis of an intelligent creator who imparts both biological information and mental capacities. These points will later be reframed through the informational model (e.g. consciousness as receiving the "broadcast" of the divine mind ).

**Chapter 7: Moral Order and Evil -- Ethical Evidence in Balance**

Chapter 7 tackles the **moral dimension**: the existence of objective moral values and duties, and the challenge posed by evil and suffering. We begin with the case for **objective moral values (Moral Objectivity)** as evidence for God. The classical moral argument, succinctly formulated by philosopher *William L. Craig*, asserts: *"If God does not exist, objective moral values and duties would not exist. But they do exist. Therefore, God exists."* . We examine this argument academically, citing meta-ethical positions. Notably, even some atheist philosophers recognize a problem in grounding objective morality naturalistically: *J. L. Mackie* called such values "queer" entities in a purely material world, and philosopher *Michael Ruse* admits that, under evolutionary naturalism, morality is ultimately an illusion engineered for survival, "*and any deeper meaning is illusory*" . We present these testimonies (Mackie, Ruse, etc.) to show that without a transcendent anchor, claims of real right and wrong lack foundation . In contrast, theism offers a coherent basis: if God is perfectly good and the creator of humans, our moral intuitions can reflect *real* duties (God's commands) and values (God's nature as the Good) . Thus, the widespread human conviction that some things (e.g. torturing children) are objectively wrong provides positive evidence for a moral lawgiver in our probabilistic model. The second part of this chapter confronts the **problem of evil (EV)**. Traditionally, the existence of gratuitous suffering is seen as strong evidence *against* God. We outline this reasoning and acknowledge its emotional force. However, we then introduce theological frameworks (with academic sources) that complicate this simple negation. The **"soul-making" theodicy**, for example, posits that a world containing challenges and suffering can serve an ennobling purpose -- facilitating the development of virtues like courage, empathy, and spiritual maturity . *John Hick's* influential work is cited: a world with no possibility of pain would, in Hick's words, leave us as "innocent, immature creatures," never able to grow morally . We also describe the **free will defense** (following *Plantinga* and others) wherein moral evil is a consequence of granting creatures freedom -- a greater good that even an omnipotent God might not logically guarantee without the risk of misuse. Additionally, **skeptical theism** is presented: the epistemological stance that we humans, with our finite perspective, cannot expect to grasp God's reasons for permitting each instance of suffering . Collectively, these ideas suggest that the observed mix of good and evil might not actually contradict God's existence; some evils could be a necessary byproduct of a world designed for higher ends . In our logistic model, after reconsideration, the "EV" variable was treated as *neutral* (zero or near-zero weight) rather than strongly negative, on the grounds that the mere presence of evil does not drastically lower the probability of God once plausible divine reasons are factored in . This chapter thus balances the moral ledger: a robust **moral law** phenomenon that favors theism, and the reality of evil which, when interpreted through rigorous theodicy, may not overturn the case. Academic sources from both sides (theistic philosophers and secular ethicists) are cited to ensure a fair and **rigorous** treatment of this most challenging aspect of theology.

**Chapter 8: Religious Experience -- The Personal and Social Evidence**

In this chapter, we investigate **religious experience (RE)** as an evidential category. Religious experiences range from mystical unity, visions, and a sense of the divine presence to more quotidian spiritual feelings. Philosophically, we invoke *Richard Swinburne's* Principle of Credulity which contends that (in the absence of defeaters) people's testimony of experiencing X is prima facie evidence of X -- applied here: if many people experience "God," that is evidence God exists, unless we have a strong reason to dismiss all such experiences. Empirically, we document the prevalence and patterns of religious experiences across cultures. Large-scale surveys indicate that such experiences are far from rare or confined to the fanatic few. For example, a Pew Forum study found that **nearly half** of Americans reported having a mystical or religious experience at least once, a number that has grown significantly from the 1960s . Even among more secular populations, a non-trivial fraction reports experiences they regard as encounters with a transcendent reality . We discuss how these experiences often share common features (a sense of peace, unity, encounter with an "Other") and how cross-cultural studies (e.g. *William James*, *Alister Hardy* archives) have catalogued their effects, typically life-changing and perceived as veridical by experiencers. The chapter also reviews neuroscientific research into spirituality (e.g. the so-called "God spot" in the brain, or neurochemical triggers of mystical states) to address whether such experiences might have natural explanations. While neuroscience can correlate brain states with spiritual experiences, it does not necessarily debunk the content -- a point we illustrate with the analogy that perception of the physical world also has brain correlates yet is generally reliable. We acknowledge the **evidential value** of religious experience is often deemed "subjective"; in our logistic model it was given a smaller weight than cosmology or morality, reflecting that those who lack such experiences may remain unconvinced . Still, we argue that *en masse*, reports of religious experience constitute a significant datum. They align with what we'd expect if humans are naturally oriented to communicate with a divine reality (as many religions teach), whereas it's somewhat surprising on strong atheism that so many brains would misfire in this way. Sociological data and psychological studies (including both supportive and skeptical perspectives) are cited to keep the discussion scholarly. By the end of Chapter 8, we conclude that while religious experiences **alone** may not prove anything, their **frequency and character** across humanity are a piece of the cumulative case -- providing *"corroborative personal evidence for those who have them"* and even *"modest statistical evidence to others,"* in the words of our prior analysis . This justifies a modest positive inclusion of RE in the probabilistic model.

**Chapter 10: The Divine Logos -- God as the Source of Information**

Marking a turn to the second layer of our inquiry, Chapter 10 revisits the ancient concept of **Logos** and connects it to contemporary notions of **information**. We begin with intellectual history: in Greek philosophy, *Logos* denoted the rational principle or reason structuring the cosmos (Heraclitus, the Stoics), and the early Christian tradition embraced this in the opening of the Gospel of John: "In the beginning was the Word (Logos), and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." We interpret this not merely theologically but philosophically -- as an assertion that **ultimate reality is mental or rational in nature**. Many theologians (from *Augustine* to *Maximus the Confessor*) have held that all of creation is suffused with divine intelligibility. We then introduce the modern translation of this idea into the language of information theory. Drawing on the work of thinkers like *John Archibald Wheeler*, *Paul Davies*, and philosopher of information *Luciano Floridi*, we propose that seeing God as the **source of information** provides a unifying metaphysical framework. A key point is clarifying what we mean by "information." We cite *John F. Haught* who notes that in the broadest sense, *"information can mean whatever gives form, order, pattern, or identity to something"* . This encompasses far more than computer bits -- it is the principle of organization in nature. The chapter argues that if God exists, God could be understood as the **ultimate informer** or architect of form: the one who imposes the laws of physics, the structures of life, the moral order, etc., onto an otherwise chaotic or indeterminate substrate. We connect this idea to Galileo's observation from Chapter 2 -- the universe is written in a mathematical language -- now interpreting the "language" as literally information encoded in reality . Just as human minds use information to convey meaning and create technology, the divine mind may instantiate information to create an ordered world. We support this with modern scientific inklings: physics increasingly speaks of information (e.g., entropy is missing information, black hole information paradox, quantum information theory). We discuss Wheeler's *"It from Bit"* hypothesis in depth: he suggested that every physical "it" (particle, field, event) at root derives from bits of binary information, and famously said all things physical are information-theoretic in origin . If one takes that seriously, it naturally raises the question: information from *where* or *whom*? We posit that a theistic answer -- an eternal Mind as the **radiant source of information** -- is a coherent explanatory extension. This chapter lays out the philosophical foundation for informational theology, equating the theological concept of Logos with the idea that *the fundamental reality is akin to mind and information rather than matter*. By reinterpreting "God" in this light, we prepare to show how this view can elegantly **explain the evidences** seen earlier (indeed, we foreshadow how fine-tuning, etc., will be seen as traces of informational input). The tone remains academic: we reference both classical sources and cutting-edge works (such as essays in *Information and the Nature of Reality: From Physics to Metaphysics*) to demonstrate that this Logos-as-information concept is taken seriously in scholarly discourse .

**Chapter 11: Information and the Cosmos -- Physics, Mathematics, and Mind**

Chapter 11 provides a deeper scientific context for the informational model, showing its resonance with **modern physics and cosmology**. We start by examining the role of information in physics: for instance, *Shannon's information theory* tells us that any ordered, low-entropy state carries more information (in the sense of being special among possibilities) . The highly ordered early universe (with its incredibly low entropy) can thus be seen as a state of immense information "injection". *Sir Roger Penrose's* observation, mentioned earlier, that the Big Bang's order was extraordinarily improbably high , is reframed: one can say the Big Bang was seeded with a huge amount of information to allow structured complexity later . Next, we discuss how laws of nature themselves can be viewed as information. We quote Eugene Wigner's famous puzzlement over the "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics" and recall Galileo's insight that geometry and math are nature's language . This mathematical elegance suggests that the universe behaves as if following an algorithm or code. If so, it is natural to think of a **Cosmic Programmer** who wrote the code (this metaphor is used carefully, distinguishing it from simulation hypothesis, although we acknowledge that idea as a secular cousin -- the notion we live in a simulation is essentially a re-imagination of the Creator as a programmer). We incorporate Wheeler's *participatory universe* idea here as well: he conjectured not only "it from bit" but that observers (conscious entities) are necessary to "bring forth" the physical reality by observing it . While controversial, this aligns with the view of reality as information: consciousness reads the information. We also highlight instances where physicists openly use quasi-theological language: e.g., *James Jeans* (as quoted earlier) and *John Barrow* speculating that the universe is a gigantic information-processing system. A subsection deals with quantum mechanics: we explain how quantum information (qubits, entanglement) has led some to interpret that **information is more fundamental than particles**. Notably, quantum teleportation and the conservation of information in physical processes bolster the view that physical reality never destroys information -- a hint that information might be an indestructible "substance" of the universe, paralleling theological claims about the eternal Logos. The chapter also examines the role of the **observer**: if consciousness collapses wavefunctions (in some interpretations), then the universe's existence of definite states might require cosmic observation -- potentially provided by an ever-present divine consciousness (this is speculative, but we note it as a provocative alignment of quantum physics with theism often discussed in the literature). By the chapter's end, readers see that the idea of God as information is *not* a fanciful add-on, but something that naturally dovetails with trends in fundamental physics. As one summary, we echo *Wheeler's* quote: *"every item of the physical world has at bottom... an immaterial source and explanation... that all things physical are information-theoretic in origin"* . Such statements from renowned physicists give credence to our model's core intuition: **material reality originates from an immaterial informing principle**, which is essentially what classical theology calls God. We thus prepare to merge this scientific perspective with the specific evidences, reinterpreting them through the information lens in the next chapter.

**Chapter 12: Reinterpreting the Evidence -- An Informational Theological Synthesis**

Chapter 12 serves as the bridge between the book's two parts, by showing that the very evidences we analyzed probabilistically in Part II can be *re-understood* in terms of the informational model from Part III. We systematically go through each evidential category and provide an enriched interpretation consistent with the idea of God as the **informational substrate of reality** . For example:

- **Fine-Tuning (FT)**: Rather than merely an anthropic oddity, the precise constants and laws appear as *"encoded cosmological parameters"* -- that is, deliberate information input specifying how the universe should operate . The fine-tuning thus reflects the "code" or *calibration* choice of the divine Logos, analogous to how a programmer selects the right values for a simulation to achieve a desired outcome.

- **Moral Objectivity (MO)**: Objective moral truths can be seen as *meta-information* infused into human minds or into the fabric of society -- a sort of moral law written on our hearts (as some theological traditions hold). In our model, God as the source of information would naturally also be the source of *ethical information*, structuring the universe not just physically but morally . Thus, when humans intuit moral truths, they are "downloading" or resonating with the Logos's moral order.

- **Consciousness (CG)**: We propose that consciousness is the **receiver** in the grand informational picture. If God is continuously broadcasting meaningful order, then conscious beings are the ones with the capacity to tune in. The chapter uses the metaphor (drawn from our Epilogue) of human minds as finite nodes that can receive and interpret the divine signal . This aligns with views in panpsychist philosophy or *Teilhard de Chardin's* ideas, but we stay within a theistic framework: consciousness doesn't create the information, it *receives* it, suggesting a pre-existing transmitter (God).

- **Religious Experience (RE)**: Continuing the analogy, religious experiences are like moments of especially clear signal reception or "resonance" with the divine information field . What the experiencer calls an encounter with God can be interpreted as their mind attuning to the broadcast of the Logos. This explains why such experiences often convey insights or a sense of profound meaning -- in our model, they are genuine taps into the source of meaning.

- **Evil (EV)**: Perhaps counterintuitively, even the existence of evil can be reframed. In informational terms, we describe evil as *"a distortion or misalignment in the informational channel"* . Rather than a thing created by God, evil is like noise that interferes with the intended signal. This could be tied to the idea of free agents (humans) introducing chaos by not following the "divine signal" (moral law), thus producing static. Theologically, God's providence then is seen as working to overcome noise with signal (e.g., through redemption, conscience, etc.). This approach doesn't erase the pain of evil, but conceptually it casts God not as the author of evil, but as the source of good signal that can be corrupted.

- **Biological Complexity (BC)**: Life's information (DNA, etc.) is straightforwardly an instance of encoded information. We suggest that if God is the author of information, then the origin of life's genetic code was a direct act of informational injection -- in other words, the DNA alphabet is part of the "program" God wrote into nature. This harmonizes with the improbability noted earlier: such complex specified information is exactly what we'd expect if a cosmic Mind implemented it, and extremely surprising to get by accident.

- **Quantum/Metaphysical Hints (QM & MN)**: The chapter also touches on the "extra" variables. Quantum phenomena (like entanglement or the appearance of observer-dependence) become less mysterious if we imagine the universe as an interconnected information network sustained by God -- non-local correlations could be seen as the unity of information under a single mind. Metaphysical necessity (the need for a necessary being) by definition points to something beyond contingent information -- in our model, the necessary being is precisely the ultimate information source that exists by its own nature.

Throughout these reinterpretations, we cite relevant scholarly voices to show we are not alone in such thinking. For instance, we reference *Information Philosopher* ideas and the concept of a "unified information field" in spirituality. The effect of this chapter is a **unification**: the probabilistic evidences are not an unrelated assortment, but rather all facets of one picture -- a universe suffused by structured information originating from a singular divine Mind . The evidences thus serve as "signals" or "footprints" of the Logos. By reframing them, this chapter also addresses potential conflicts: any apparent disharmony between science and faith is eased since both are now speaking about information (science maps how information behaves in nature, theology claims to identify its source). The language of mathematics, morality, and consciousness all cohere under the information paradigm. This sets up the final discussion: why combining the empirical and the informational approaches yields a more coherent understanding than either alone.

**Chapter 13: Implications for Theology and Philosophy -- God's Nature in an Informational World**

Having outlined the informational reinterpretation, we now explore its implications for classical theology and metaphysics. This chapter asks: if God is understood as *"the **informational substrate of existence** -- the encoder of intelligibility, the emitter of structured order"* , how does this relate to traditional attributes of God and doctrinal views? We examine compatibility with the idea of a personal God: information alone might sound impersonal, but we clarify that information in our thesis is always **information from a Mind**. Thus God is still personal, intentional, and free -- not a blind data-stream. We revisit attributes like omnipotence and omniscience: omnipotence can be framed as the power to instantiate any logically possible information (write any code consistent with non-contradiction), and omniscience as having complete information about all states of reality. Interestingly, this model casts **creation** not so much as a one-time act of making matter, but an ongoing **communication**: *"the universe is not only created but communicated. Every law, every structure, every moral insight... becomes a kind of revelation -- the unveiling of God not in thunder, but in syntax,"* as our Epilogue poeticly put it . We discuss how this continuous communication aligns with notions of God's immanence (present in the world) combined with transcendence (as the source beyond it). We also address the concept of miracles in an informational universe: rather than violations of laws, miracles might be seen as *new information inputs* at opportune moments (tweaking the "code" in rare instances), which is conceivable if God is the author of the system -- though predictably such interventions would be sparse to maintain regularity. Philosophically, we engage with questions of **meaning** and **value**: if reality at bottom is meaningful information, then meaning is not an accidental byproduct of blind matter but built into the cosmos (fulfilling the hopes of existentialists and theologians alike). We also consider human purpose: in this model, creatures made "in the image of God" would be co-creators and co-communicators of information (through rationality, creativity, love), thus bridging theology and information theory in anthropology. Potential criticisms are discussed, such as whether calling God "information" is just metaphor -- we respond that it is an analogy, but one grounded in rigorous parallels between theological doctrine and information science. Finally, we highlight that this informational ontology resonates with strands of process theology, panentheism (God in all, all in God in terms of informational presence), and even classical Thomism (where God is ipsum esse subsistens -- one could analogously say God is self-subsistent information giving being to all things). The aim is to show that our model enriches theological dialogue rather than overturning it. By framing age-old doctrines in a new light, we make them conversant with contemporary science and philosophy. This chapter closes by reinforcing that conceiving of God as **Logos/Information** fulfills the intuitive sense expressed by figures like Galileo, Kepler, Einstein -- that a *Mind comprehensible through mathematics and reason pervades reality*. It thereby invites both believers and skeptics to look at the world as a place *"where the evidence for God is woven into the very fabric of information that sustains existence"* , potentially transforming how we find divine meaning in scientific discovery.

**Chapter 14: Conclusion -- Unifying Probability and Information in the Search for God**

The final chapter synthesizes and reflects on the **unified rationale** behind this two-model approach, solidifying why combining the probabilistic evidence-based framework with the informational-theological framework provides a more coherent understanding than either could alone. We reiterate that the **logistic regression model** offered a disciplined way to weigh diverse evidences and demonstrated that belief in God can be backed by a **strong cumulative case**, approaching the question with scientific and statistical rigor. However, that model by itself only addresses the likelihood of *some* transcendent reality corresponding to those evidences -- it does not tell us much about the *nature* of that reality. The **informational model**, on the other hand, provides a rich metaphysical narrative in which those evidences make sense: it portrays a cosmos where everything from physical laws to conscious minds are manifestations of an underlying informational principle (Logos). By itself, the informational model is elegant, but one might worry it's speculative -- that's where the empirical evidence comes in to ground it. **Together, the two layers reinforce each other**: the probabilistic model establishes that it is rational to believe in a God given the evidence, and the informational model explains why those evidences would obtain if indeed such a God exists. We explicitly highlight examples of this synergy: Fine-tuning was used in Part II to increase God's probability; in Part III we saw that if God is an informational creator, fine-tuning is exactly what we'd expect (a calibrated universe). Consciousness was treated as evidence; in the info paradigm, consciousness is a built-in feature for receiving God's communication, thus its evidential force is elucidated. In effect, the informational worldview functions as a **theoretical lens** through which the empirical data becomes more than a list of curiosities -- it becomes a coherent story of a world informed by divinity. We also argue that this interdisciplinary integration has a broader epistemological significance. It exemplifies how science and theology can interact fruitfully: not in conflict or in isolated silos, but in a dialogue where scientific findings inform theological conceptualization (e.g., using information theory to refine our idea of God) and theological ideas inspire new interpretations of scientific facts (e.g., seeing meaning in mathematical laws). This model of dialogue aligns with what philosopher *Ian Barbour* called the "integration" model of science and religion, here achieved through the concrete case of evidences for God. The conclusion addresses possible remaining criticisms -- for example, could the approach be accused of *confirmation bias* (cherry-picking evidence)? We respond that we have intentionally included a wide range of evidence, even the traditionally negative (problem of evil), and subjected everything to analytic scrutiny. The unified model does not force a belief; rather it shows that both **heart and mind** -- empirical observation and intuitive metaphysical insight -- point in the same direction. We close with a forward-looking reflection: **if God is indeed the ultimate information, then every pursuit of knowledge (science, math, philosophy) is, in a sense, an encounter with the divine.** Echoing once more the metaphor of God as "broadcaster," we suggest that learning to listen to the many channels of evidence and meaning in the universe is a spiritual as well as intellectual journey. In sum, the coherent academic monograph we have designed demonstrates that one can rigorously calculate the probability of God and concurrently articulate a vision of God that illuminates why the world is as we find it. This dual approach aspires to fulfill both the **head's demand for evidence** and the **heart's intuition of an intelligible cosmos**, uniting them in a single narrative -- that of a universe written by a divine Author in the living language of mathematics and information . The book ends by encouraging further scholarship at this fruitful intersection of probability theory, physics, and theology, and inviting the reader to ponder the personal implications of living in a world that may very well be *"God's spoken Word"* in informational form .
