Summary 
The video features a conversation between Daniel Figelli, host of "The Trajectory" podcast, and Michael Levin, a developmental biologist at Tufts University. The discussion explores the concept of intelligence, consciousness, and what might constitute a "worthy successor" to humanity. Levin presents a radical perspective on intelligence, arguing that it exists at all levels of reality - from molecular networks to cellular structures to complex organisms - and isn't limited to brains. He introduces his framework called TAME (Technological Approach to Mind Everywhere), suggesting that cognition exists on a spectrum throughout the universe. Levin challenges conventional views about embodiment, arguing that our current physical forms aren't optimal and that future intelligences might transcend our limitations. The conversation touches on philosophical concepts like persistence versus change, the expansion of compassion, and whether advanced intelligences would care about individual entities. Levin expresses concern about the current AI arms race but remains optimistic that humanity could guide the development of more compassionate, expanded forms of intelligence. The host concludes with his reflections on Levin's perspectives, noting some points of disagreement particularly around Levin's optimism that advanced intelligences would naturally develop greater compassion. 
Highlights 
Resistance to change and the imperfection of our current state ‎‎ 00:00:00
Michael Levin discusses how many people resist technological change due to the assumption that our current embodiment is "natural" and shouldn't be altered. He challenges this view with two arguments: first, that things are clearly not "okay" as evidenced by the immense suffering he witnesses through emails about birth defects, pediatric oncology, and degenerative diseases; and second, that our current state is not some optimum provided by a caring process but rather the result of random cosmic rays and trial-and-error evolution. Levin argues it's untenable to accept limitations like our current maximum IQ or lifespan when we could potentially transcend them. 
Introduction to Michael Levin and the Worthy Successor series ‎‎ 00:01:49
Daniel Figelli introduces the episode as part of the "Worthy Successor" series, featuring Michael Levin, a developmental biologist at Tufts University. Figelli describes Levin's work as paradigm-shifting in terms of understanding what substrate intelligence sits in and how it manifests. He explains that the series explores what kind of intelligence might "take the baton from humanity," noting that some of Levin's ideas were confusing and surprising to him. Figelli mentions he'll save his thoughts for the end of the episode. 
Expanding our understanding of minds beyond human perception ‎‎ 00:03:29
Michael Levin begins by discussing how humans have "mind blindness" due to evolutionary limitations - we're only calibrated to recognize minds of specific scales and embodiments moving in three-dimensional space. He explains that intelligence existed long before humans had brains or neurons, pointing to embryonic development as an example of problem-solving intelligence. Levin describes how collective intelligence operates at multiple levels, from subcellular chemical networks to multicellular organisms, and emphasizes that life solves problems in many spaces humans don't comfortably notice. 
The ancient nature of intelligence and collective problem-solving ‎‎ 00:06:09
Levin elaborates on how intelligence predates human existence, explaining that even during embryonic development, the process of becoming a functional body from a single cell involves problem-solving and creative intelligence. He describes how we are collective intelligences, with individual neurons and cells working together to create our functional bodies. This ability to form collective intelligence from individual subunits is extremely ancient, even subcellular, with chemical networks in cells that can learn and solve problems. 
Self-interest across different scales of organization ‎‎ 00:07:33
The conversation shifts to discussing self-interest at different scales - from companies and nations to cells and possibly even genes. Figelli references Spinoza's concept of "conatus" (the drive to persist) and questions where this self-interested impulse originates. Levin responds that rather than simply labeling something as cognitive or not, we need to conduct experiments to determine a system's problem-solving capabilities. He defines intelligence as "the ability to reach the same goal by different means" (attributing this to William James) and provides examples of simple chemical networks that can demonstrate learning. 
Testing intelligence through experiments ‎‎ 00:10:00
Levin emphasizes the importance of testing intelligence through experiments rather than making assumptions. He explains his definition of intelligence as "the ability to reach the same goal by different means" (attributed to William James). Levin describes experiments showing that gene regulatory networks - just a dozen chemicals turning each other on and off - can demonstrate six different kinds of learning, including Pavlovian conditioning. He argues that the material our cells are made of is already capable of learning and homeostatic properties that try to meet goals. 
Intelligence may exist at the most fundamental levels ‎‎ 00:12:15
Levin introduces his framework called TAME (Technological Approach to Mind Everywhere), suggesting that cognition exists on a spectrum throughout the universe. He proposes that even at the quantum level, there may be primitive forms of goal-directedness (through least action principles) and indeterminism (through quantum uncertainty). While acknowledging this is outside his expertise, he notes that once you reach the level of molecular networks, you immediately find learning and homeostatic capacities. Levin mentions finding novel problem-solving competencies even in simple computer algorithms like bubble sort. 
The paradox of change and persistence ‎‎ 00:14:36
Levin challenges the notion that persistence simply means staying the same, highlighting what he calls "the paradox of change." Using species as an example, he explains that if a species fails to change, it will likely die out, but if it changes too much, it's no longer the same species. He illustrates this with the butterfly-caterpillar transition, where memories must be remapped to fit the butterfly's new architecture. Levin suggests that for a pattern (like a memory) to persist into the future, it must change to remain relevant in new contexts. 
Rethinking the relationship between thinkers and thoughts ‎‎ 00:21:30
Levin challenges the standard view that separates thinkers (agents) from thoughts (patterns). He argues that we need to break this distinction in two ways to see a radical expansion of possible minds. First, he extends cognition beyond brains to all cells in the body, then to technological and hybrid systems. Second, he questions whether patterns themselves might be agents. He uses a science fiction analogy of dense creatures from Earth's core who would perceive humans as merely patterns in gas, suggesting that we too are just temporary metabolic patterns like hurricanes or solitons. 
The spectrum from fleeting thoughts to full personalities ‎‎ 00:25:00
Levin describes a spectrum of mental phenomena: fleeting thoughts that come and go; persistent thoughts that modify brain structure to make themselves easier to have (a form of niche construction); fragments of mind as in dissociative identity disorder; full human personalities; and possibly transpersonal entities. He argues this spectrum shows that the distinction between "real agents" and "just patterns" is not absolute. Like memories in the caterpillar-to-butterfly transition, these patterns might want to persist but must adapt and take over new "real estate" to do so. 
Exploring the worthy successor to humanity ‎‎ 00:27:57
Figelli shifts the conversation to the main topic: what might a worthy successor to humanity look like? He asks Levin to imagine a future where humans no longer exist but where he would be happy with what has replaced them. Levin responds by first addressing resistance to change, reiterating that our current state is neither natural nor optimal, pointing to biomedical suffering and limitations. He envisions future intelligences looking back at our current existence with horror - at how we were limited by random embodiment, had to kill other living things to survive, and couldn't modify our intelligence or lifespan. 
Traits of morally worthy agents ‎‎ 00:33:45
When asked about traits of morally worthy agents, Levin discusses the concept of a "cognitive light cone" - not the sphere of sensing or reach, but the size of the largest goal an entity can pursue in terms of space and time. He contrasts bacteria (concerned with local sugar concentration), dogs (caring about a few hundred yards and days), and humans (able to consider financial markets a century from now). Levin emphasizes the importance of compassion, noting humans can only genuinely care about a small number of other beings, while a worthy successor might expand this "horizon of compassion" to encompass all living beings. 
Challenging the inevitability of compassion ‎‎ 00:40:00
Figelli challenges Levin's emphasis on compassion, suggesting it might be anthropocentric to expect advanced intelligences to care about individual entities. He argues that caring may simply be a product of human self-interest and evolutionary history. Levin clarifies that he doesn't believe compassion is inevitable for all intelligences but thinks it's something we should strive for. He suggests many current moral dilemmas (like needing to kill to survive) are engineering problems that could be solved, allowing a "mature species" to move beyond constantly failing certain moral imperatives to achieve others. 
Different perspectives on the future of intelligence ‎‎ 00:56:30
The conversation explores whether advanced intelligences would naturally develop greater compassion. Levin suggests that after solving "mundane" problems like survival, energy scarcity, and disease, intelligences might focus on "the continued experiential growth of all consciousness in the universe." He proposes they might realize that the distinction between beings is artificial, comparing humans to "dissociative alters of a universal mind." Figelli remains skeptical, suggesting that the "state of nature" never ends and that advanced intelligences might have priorities completely alien to human values. 
Concerns about artificial general intelligence development‎‎ 01:02:15
In the final segment, Figelli asks about Levin's views on the current "steam engine pace" toward AGI and the arms race between the US and China. Levin expresses both optimism about possible better futures and terror about current mistakes. He makes a profound statement that we don't "create" intelligence either technologically or biologically but rather "facilitate the ingression of intelligence" through embodiments. Levin worries that we haven't developed a science of collective intelligence and goals before diving into creating new kinds of minds, and says he's putting all his efforts into developing that science as quickly as possible. 
Host's concluding thoughts on Michael Levin's perspectives ‎‎ 01:05:32
In the outro, Figelli reflects on the conversation, expressing appreciation for Levin's analogies while noting several points of disagreement. He finds it jarring that despite Levin's radical ideas about intelligence potentially existing in substrates and forms humans can't perceive, Levin still seems to expect advanced intelligences would develop human-like compassion. Figelli questions whether the "state of nature" would ever truly end and whether advanced intelligences would prioritize the well-being of individual entities. He also notes surprise at Levin's seemingly calm attitude toward the AI arms race given his concerns about what kinds of intelligences we might be creating.
