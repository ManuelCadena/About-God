Summary 
This interview mainly discussed Dr. Michael Levin's groundbreaking work on xenobots and bioelectricity. The host begins by introducing Dr. Levin as one of the most innovative scientists alive, highlighting his creation of xenobots - the world's first living programmable organisms made from frog cells. Dr. Levin explains that xenobots are self-assembling, motile organisms created from frog epithelial cells that exhibit behaviors different from their original purpose. He discusses how these organisms demonstrate unexpected behaviors like kinematic replication (creating copies of themselves) and responding to sound stimuli. The conversation explores the cognitive capabilities of these simple cellular structures, with Dr. Levin arguing that cognition exists on a spectrum that extends far beyond neural systems. He challenges conventional views by suggesting that cells and tissues possess forms of intelligence, memory, and problem-solving abilities. The interview also covers his work on anthrobots (made from human tracheal cells) that can heal neural tissue wounds, and his developing theory of consciousness that radically proposes consciousness may be fundamental to the universe rather than something created or emergent. Dr. Levin emphasizes that his research has profound implications for regenerative medicine, diverse intelligence recognition, and our ethical frameworks for interacting with non-human minds.

Is there anything specific about the Xenopus frog, or could you make xenobots from other species? ‎‎ 00:41:29
I don't believe there's anything unique about the frog system. We used it because it's very tractable and easy to use. Do you think xenobots have consciousness? ‎‎ 00:49:45
I have not claimed and don't think anybody on our team has claimed anything about consciousness for these things. Consciousness is obviously a very difficult issue. Typically, there are three kinds of reasons people use to attribute consciousness to others: behavior (goal-directed behavior, preferences, learning), architecture (being made of the same materials as conscious beings), and evolutionary lineage. Using any of these criteria, what I've claimed about consciousness in general is that for the exact same reasons you attribute consciousness to other beings, you should be very serious about attributing it to other organs and tissues of your body. I absolutely think that xenobots have an inner perspective, though we don't have data on this and it's very hard to test. My suspicion is that of course they do, but I think experience is extremely widely distributed across the universe. What I can't tell you is what kind of consciousness they have, what they're aware of, or what their valence is - we don't have those data yet. 
Can you share a bit about your theory of consciousness? ‎‎ 00:56:32
My framework is disturbing on both ends of the spectrum. I am more radical than those who extend consciousness down to insects, plants, or single cells. I don't think there's anything special about being proteinaceous or having evolved through mutation and selection. I don't think we make minds or create consciousness - not when we make an embryo or an AI system. I think we facilitate the ingression of certain patterns from a platonic space. In very simple systems that are not alive, not evolved, and deterministic, you already get surprised behaviors and cognitive capacities. For the same reasons that the laws of chemistry don't tell the whole story of a human mind, the laws of computer science don't tell the whole story of even simple machines. We've found that even sorting algorithms - simple, deterministic, six lines of code - do things that are nowhere in the algorithm, like little "side quests." This is an extremely minimal model of what we see in life: you're subject to the laws of physics and chemistry, but you do things that, while not prohibited by those laws, are not prescribed by them either.
