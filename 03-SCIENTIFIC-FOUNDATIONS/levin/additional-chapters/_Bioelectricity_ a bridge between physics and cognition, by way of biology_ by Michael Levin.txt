Summary 
The video presents a lecture by a researcher on bioelectricity as a bridge between physics and cognition. The speaker explores how mental goals translate into physical actions through bioelectric signaling at various levels of biological organization. They explain that our bodies are made of "agential materials" - cells with their own competencies and problem-solving abilities. The lecture demonstrates how tissues store "memories" in electrical patterns that guide development and regeneration, showing examples like axolotl limb regeneration and planarian head regrowth. The speaker presents research on manipulating these bioelectric patterns to create new structures, such as inducing eye formation in unexpected locations or changing the head shape of flatworms. The presentation introduces "anthrobots" - synthetic life forms created from human cells that can self-assemble and perform tasks like neural wound repair. The speaker concludes by discussing the ethical implications of creating novel biological entities and the need to develop principles for relating to beings that don't share our evolutionary history. 
Highlights 
Bioelectricity as a Bridge Between Physics and Cognition ‎‎ 00:00:04
The speaker introduces the concept of bioelectricity as a bridge between physics and cognition. They point out how our abstract mental goals (financial, social, research) must translate into physical actions through charged molecules crossing muscle membranes. This represents the "miracle of everyday voluntary motion" - how chemistry in our bodies is controlled by thoughts and intentions. The speaker questions where the interface between cognition and physics occurs, suggesting that rather than a distinct boundary, "it's cognition all the way down" from high-level minds to molecules. 
Agential Materials and Multi-Scale Competency ‎‎ 00:02:45
The speaker introduces the concept that we are made of "agential materials" - materials with agendas and competencies. They show a single-celled organism called Lacrymaria to demonstrate how even without a brain, these cells display remarkable competency in their environment. The speaker explains that throughout evolutionary history, biology has been solving problems in many different spaces beyond just three-dimensional physical space, including gene expression, physiological states, and anatomical states. They introduce the "multi-scale competency architecture" - the idea that at every level of organization, from molecular networks to collections of organisms, there are agendas and problem-solving capabilities. 
Regeneration and Pattern Memory in Organisms ‎‎ 00:05:06
The speaker presents the axolotl, an amphibian with remarkable regenerative abilities that can replace damaged limbs, eyes, and organs. They explain how cells know when to stop regenerating - when the correct anatomical structure has been completed. The speaker describes an experiment where a tail grafted to the side of an amphibian transforms into a limb, demonstrating how local cells obey a global anatomical plan. They question where the information for complex anatomical structures comes from, noting it's not directly encoded in DNA but emerges from "physiological software" running on genetically specified hardware. 
Bioelectric Patterns and Developmental Memory ‎‎ 00:08:33
The speaker explains how cells throughout the body use electrical networks similar to those in the brain. They show how early embryos have electrical patterns that serve as scaffolds for future development, calling this the "electric face" - a decoded electrical memory of what a proper face should look like. The speaker describes their research tools for reading these bioelectric patterns using voltage-sensitive fluorescent dyes and computational modeling. They explain that their approach doesn't use external electromagnetic fields but instead hacks the normal interface cells use to control each other's behavior through ion channels and electrical synapses. 
Rewriting Pattern Memories to Create New Structures ‎‎ 00:12:15
The speaker demonstrates how they can rewrite bioelectric pattern memories to induce the formation of whole organs in new locations. They show an example where injecting ion channel RNA creates a spot of voltage that instructs cells to build an eye in an unexpected location. The speaker emphasizes that they're not micromanaging gene expression or controlling specific cells, but rather giving high-level instructions to competent tissue. They also show how manipulating electrical gradients in planarian flatworms can change the shape of their regenerated heads to resemble different species. 
Anthrobots: Synthetic Life Forms from Human Cells ‎‎ 00:16:37
The speaker introduces "anthrobots" - synthetic life forms made from adult human tracheal epithelial cells that self-assemble into swimming organisms. Despite being 100% human cells, these structures don't resemble any stage of human development. The speaker explains that when freed from the influence of the body, these cells can form entirely new structures with different behaviors. They demonstrate how anthrobots can find and repair neural wounds by landing in damaged areas and helping to knit neurons back together. The speaker notes that these cells, which normally deal with mucus and particles in the trachea, can become completely different creatures with 9,000 differently expressed genes. 
Future of Biomedicine and Ethical Considerations ‎‎ 00:20:16
The speaker concludes by discussing the implications of their research for the future of biomedicine. They suggest that future medicine will look more like "somatic psychiatry" than chemistry - focusing on collaborating with and convincing the various intelligences in our bodies. The speaker addresses ethical and social considerations, noting that the natural forms described by Darwin represent only a tiny corner of the possible space of viable embodied minds. They predict we'll increasingly live with cyborgs, hybrids, and novel combinations of evolved and engineered materials, requiring new ethical principles for relating to beings that don't share our evolutionary history.
