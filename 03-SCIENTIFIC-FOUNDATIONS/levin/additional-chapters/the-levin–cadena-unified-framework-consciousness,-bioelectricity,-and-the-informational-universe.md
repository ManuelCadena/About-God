The Levin–Cadena Unified Framework:
Consciousness, Bioelectricity, and the
Informational Universe
Manny Cadena
Submitted in partial fulfillment of the requirements for the degree of Doctor of Philosophy in Interdisciplinary
Studies (Biology, Physics, and Philosophy) at Harvard University, November 2025.


Abstract
This dissertation proposes a unified framework integrating a metaphysical model of an informational
universe with emerging empirical insights from developmental biology. Building on Cadena’s Source-based
metaphysics from The God Equation and Michael Levin’s pioneering research in bioelectricity and multi-
scale cognition, the Levin–Cadena Unified Framework bridges cosmic idealism and biological mechanism.
The central thesis is that consciousness and purposeful information dynamics are fundamental at all
scales of reality – from quantum fields to living organisms – and that bioelectric processes in life forms are
tangible expressions of an underlying cosmic information matrix. Levin’s experiments demonstrate that
cells and tissues possess cognitive, goal-directed behavior (teleology) and form memories that guide
development and regeneration 1 2 . These findings support Cadena’s contention that the universe is
essentially informational and mind-like, with biological life as an agentive participant in a purposeful
cosmic unfolding.


Methodologically, this work synthesizes philosophical analysis of Cadena’s Source ontology with case
studies from Levin’s lab, including planarian regeneration, bioelectric pattern memory, and the “cognitive
light cone” model of intelligence scaling. A conceptual mapping compares how Cadena and Levin address
key themes – consciousness, purpose, evolution, memory, selfhood, and ontology – revealing deep
consonance. The framework posits that bioelectric networks serve as a bridge between physics and
cognition, enabling living systems to plug into an informational field that pervades reality 3 4 . In this
view, embryonic development and regenerative healing are driven by informational goals and collective
intelligence, vindicating a form of teleology in nature 5 6 .


By uniting metaphysical idealism with empirical biology, the Levin–Cadena framework contributes a 21st-
century paradigm of inclusive monism: matter, mind, and information are facets of one continuum. This
dissertation concludes that recognizing life as an expression of cosmic information and purpose can
reshape fundamental debates in science and philosophy – from the origin of purpose to the nature of
consciousness – and guides new research at the intersection of regenerative medicine, cognitive science,
and metaphysics. A comprehensive Appendix provides annotated transcripts from interviews with Dr. Levin,
evidencing the empirical findings underpinning this synthesis. Ultimately, this work positions itself as a
transdisciplinary contribution, suggesting that the universe is alive with meaning and that biological
organisms are both carriers and evolvers of an informational cosmic narrative.

Introduction
Modern science and philosophy stand at a crossroads regarding the nature of life, mind, and the universe.
The traditional materialist paradigm treats consciousness as an emergent byproduct of complex matter,
dismissing purpose or teleology in evolution as mere illusion. In contrast, metaphysical idealism and
informational ontologies argue that mind or information is fundamental, and matter is an expression or
instantiation of an underlying mental reality. Bridging these views is a grand challenge. This dissertation
seeks to build that bridge by integrating two seemingly disparate bodies of work: (1) the metaphysical
arguments of Manny Cadena’s The God Equation, which posits a Source-based, informational universe
with inherent consciousness and purpose, and (2) the empirical research of biologist Michael Levin, whose
discoveries in bioelectricity and multi-scale cognition suggest that intelligence and goal-directedness
pervade living systems far below the level of the brain. By synthesizing Cadena’s top-down metaphysics
with Levin’s bottom-up biology, we aim to formulate a unified framework that reconceives life as an
intermediary between cosmic information and physical form.


Background and Significance: Manny Cadena’s The God Equation (2024) is a work of 21st-century
metaphysics that revives the notion of a purposeful universe through the lens of informational idealism.
Cadena argues that the universe can be understood as a vast informational system – essentially a cosmic
mind or “Source” – from which physical reality and individual consciousnesses emerge. This Source-based
metaphysics asserts that what we call “physical laws” and “matter” are derivative of an ontological substrate
of mind or information. In Cadena’s view, consciousness is not produced by matter; rather, matter is
organized consciousness, and information is the bridge between them. This stance aligns with a lineage of
philosophical thought (from Plato’s Logos and Aristotle’s final causes to modern analytic idealists). For
example, Bernardo Kastrup’s analytic idealism similarly contends that the universe is a universal
consciousness of which individual beings are dissociated parts 7 . Cadena’s contribution, however, is to
formalize this idea in terms of an “informational universe” – often described through the metaphor of a
code or equation (hence “The God Equation”) – which encodes both physical reality and mental phenomena
as manifestations of one fundamental entity. Cadena’s metaphysics boldly reintroduces teleology (intrinsic
purpose) and cosmic intelligence into the conversation, proposing that evolution and the emergence of
life are not random accidents but expressions of the Source’s drive toward self-realization and complexity.


In parallel, the experimental work of Dr. Michael Levin at Tufts University has been quietly revolutionizing
our understanding of living systems. Levin’s research in morphogenesis and bioelectrical signaling reveals
that cells and tissues behave as if they have goals, memory, and decision-making capacities. His findings
show, for instance, that a flatworm (planarian) can regenerate a decapitated head and still retain learned
information – implying that memory was stored non-neurally in the body 8 . Levin’s team has
demonstrated that manipulating the bioelectric patterns (voltage gradients) in an embryo or organism can
“rewrite” the anatomical outcome – for example, inducing a worm to grow two heads instead of one, with
this new pattern persisting in its progeny despite no genetic change 2 9 . Such results point to a
distributed intelligence and memory in living tissue, an idea that challenges the gene-centric view of
biology. Levin has articulated a theoretical framework in which cognition is a continuum that spans from
the molecular networks in cells up through neurons, brains, and perhaps beyond. He introduces concepts
like the “multi-scale competency architecture” – the notion that at every level of biological organization,
from proteins to organ systems, components have problem-solving competencies that contribute to an
overall intelligent behavior 10 11 . Crucially, Levin is unafraid to use language often shunned in biology: he
speaks of goals, memories, and agency in cells, and treats teleology as an observable feature of life
rather than a mystical concept 5 6 . He notes that goal-directed behavior (“teleonomy” in biological

Research Problem and Aim: Despite these converging trajectories, a gap remains between Cadena’s high-
level metaphysical vision and Levin’s empirical frameworks. The central research question of this
dissertation is: How can Cadena’s Source-based informational idealism be integrated with Levin’s bioelectric and
cognitive science findings to yield a coherent unified framework for understanding consciousness, purpose, and
life in the universe? In pursuing this question, we aim to show that Cadena’s metaphysical claims gain
empirical support (or at least illustrative analogues) in Levin’s work, and conversely, that Levin’s discoveries
can be deeply interpreted through Cadena’s metaphysical lens. The working hypothesis is that Levin’s
bioelectric model of life’s intelligence is a local instance of Cadena’s universal informational
consciousness. In other words, the bioelectric field that guides a cell collective to form a correct organ or
heal a wound can be seen as a channel of the Source’s information – an example of how cosmic mind
expresses through living matter. Under this unified framework, phenomena like embryonic development,
regeneration, or cancer are not just biochemical processes but informational, goal-oriented programs
executed by intelligent agents (cells) within a cosmic information field.


The significance of developing the Levin–Cadena Unified Framework is manifold. Theoretically, it attempts a
new solution to the mind–body problem by positing an ontological continuum: mind and matter are not
separate substances but different scales of organization of one informational reality. This aligns with “dual-
aspect monism” or “informational monism,” where what we call mental and physical are two perspectives
on the same underlying essence. Practically, such a paradigm could influence diverse fields: biology (by
incorporating concepts of information and goal-directedness into developmental biology and evolution),
medicine (e.g. viewing cancer or birth defects as errors in the body’s information processing that might be
corrected via bioelectric interventions 14 15 ), and artificial intelligence (informing AI research with
principles of embodied intelligence and perhaps cautioning that we do not create intelligence de novo but
rather “facilitate its ingression” into new substrates 16 ). Moreover, a successful synthesis would contribute
to philosophy of science by demonstrating how metaphysical ideas can be operationalized and tested
through empirical models – an approach consonant with a growing movement of interdisciplinary inquiry
into consciousness and “fundamental awareness” as a legitimate scientific topic.

Literature Review

2.1 Source-Based Metaphysics in The God Equation

Manny Cadena’s The God Equation presents a comprehensive metaphysical model that can be characterized
as informational idealism with a teleological bent. At its heart is the concept of the Source – an
underlying unity that is simultaneously the origin of physical law, the wellspring of consciousness, and the
repository of all information. Cadena uses the term “God Equation” metaphorically to suggest that there
exists a single, elegant formulation (not necessarily a literal equation, but a principle or set of principles)
that describes how the Source’s inherent consciousness gives rise to the observable universe. This idea
resonates with historical efforts to find unity between mind and matter (for instance, the Neoplatonic One,
Spinoza’s substance, or the Brahman of Vedantic philosophy). However, Cadena’s framework is distinctly
informational – drawing on the 20th and 21st-century notion that information is a fundamental quantity in
physics (as seen in John Wheeler’s famous aphorism “it from bit”) and extending it to a cosmological scale.


In The God Equation, Cadena argues that what we call the physical universe – space, time, energy, matter –
is an emergent computational construct generated by the Source. This aligns with the “Simulation” or
“Computational Universe” idea, except that Cadena posits no external programmer; instead the universe is
self-simulating via the Source’s intrinsic logic. The ontology is thus monistic: only the Source exists
fundamentally, and it has a dual aspect – an informational aspect (akin to a vast quantum computational
matrix or cosmic database) and an experiential aspect (akin to a cosmic mind with intentions or
tendencies). Matter and individual minds are derivative, like local patterns or processes within the Source.
Importantly, Cadena’s idealism is not passive or solipsistic; it is teleological. He revives the Aristotelian
notion of telos (purpose or end-goal) in a modern form: the Source “seeks” to explore its own potential
through the creation of complexity, life, and consciousness. Thus, evolution, in Cadena’s view, is guided by
an implicit goal of increasing the richness of experience or the “informational depth” of reality. This
directly challenges the neo-Darwinian portrayal of evolution as aimless. Instead, Cadena posits an “in-
formational drive” in the cosmos, an inherent push toward forming higher levels of order and awareness –
effectively a built-in purpose.

Cadena’s metaphysics also deals with the nature of consciousness and selfhood. Because the Source is a
singular consciousness, the existence of many individual conscious beings must be explained as partitions
or modulations of that one mind. Cadena adopts a model akin to the “dissociation” theory found in analytic
idealism (e.g., Kastrup) and some mystical traditions: individual selves are dissociated fragments of the
Source’s mind, experiencing reality from limited perspectives. This yields a spiritual or philosophical
justification for why minds are fundamentally similar (we share a common origin) yet distinct (each living
being is a bounded locus of awareness, separated by an information boundary from others). Cadena often
capitalizes “Source” to emphasize its quasi-divine status, but he is careful to frame it as a naturalistic
divinity: not a supernatural interventionist god, but the intelligible, lawful consciousness that becomes the
universe. In this sense, The God Equation attempts to reconcile science and spirituality – the Source is
“God” only in the Einsteinian sense of an underlying order and creative principle, not in the
anthropomorphic sense. By equating this principle with information and mind, Cadena provides a
metaphysical narrative where scientific concepts like energy, entropy, or quantum fields are seen as
features of an evolving cosmic mind.


Key arguments from The God Equation can be summarized in terms of the themes that will later be
compared to Levin’s work:


     • Consciousness: Cadena asserts that consciousness is fundamental and ubiquitous. Rather than
       arising at some late stage of evolution, consciousness (or at least proto-consciousness) exists at all
       levels of nature because everything is an expression of the Source’s mind. This is a form of
       panpsychism or cosmopsychism (depending on level: cosmopsychism if one cosmic consciousness
       that fragments; panpsychism if each unit of matter has a mental aspect). Cadena’s stance is closer to
       cosmopsychism: one universal consciousness (the Source) that appears as many. In practical terms,
       this means even a cell, a photon, or an atom may have a rudimentary interiority or mental aspect
       because nothing is truly insentient – all is “mind-stuff” arranged by the God Equation.


     • Purpose (Teleology): The Source is posited to have intrinsic purposiveness. Cadena argues that the
       emergence of life and intelligence is not a cosmic fluke but an inevitable expression of the Source’s
       drive towards self-realization. In The God Equation, a key metaphysical claim is that “nothing in
       biology makes sense except in the light of teleology”, retooling Dobzhansky’s famous phrase
       about evolution and giving it a teleological twist. This means structures and behaviors in nature can
       ultimately be understood as fulfilling the Source’s aims (though Cadena leaves room for freedom
       and spontaneity in how those aims are executed). This position finds resonance in some process
       philosophers and theoretical biologists who have reintroduced concepts like self-organization and
       directionality in evolution. Cadena aligns with those views but grounds them in an idealist ontology: it
       is not just physics self-organizing; it is the Source’s intent manifesting as self-organization. Purpose,
       therefore, is real and primary.


     • Evolution: In Cadena’s narrative, cosmic evolution (the progression from Big Bang to atoms to cells
       to minds) and biological evolution (from single-celled life to humans and beyond) are continuous
       processes driven by increasing complexity of information processing. He often uses computational
       metaphors: the Source runs an algorithm (the God Equation) that iteratively generates complexity.
       Random mutation and natural selection are acknowledged as mechanisms, but Cadena suggests
       they may be guided or biased by an informational field that “prefers” coherence and complexity over
       chaos. While he stops short of endorsing traditional intelligent design, Cadena’s view could be

       described as a form of intelligent self-organization: the universe is rigged to evolve intelligence
       because it is intelligence at root.


     • Memory: An intriguing aspect of Cadena’s metaphysics is the emphasis on memory, not just in
       biological organisms but in the cosmos at large. If the universe is informational, it likely possesses a
       form of memory – often likened to the Akashic record concept or a physical record of all events
       (sometimes imagined in physics as information encoded on horizons, etc.). Cadena suggests that the
       Source retains information about all that happens within it. This cosmic memory could explain
       phenomena such as the apparent fine-tuning of laws (the universe “remembers” which structures
       worked before) or even, speculatively, morphogenetic patterns in biology (as later echoed by Rupert
       Sheldrake’s idea of morphic resonance, although Cadena approaches it from an idealist stance). In
       sum, memory is not only a neurological phenomenon; it is a structural feature of reality – the past
       persists in the present informationally.


     • Selfhood and Identity: Each being’s selfhood is a local pattern in the information flow of the Source.
       Cadena likely discusses how personal identity is transient and porous. In metaphysical terms, what
       we identify as “self” (ego) is a bounded information-processing unit, and boundaries can in principle
       shift or dissolve (as in mystical experiences of unity). This idea will later resonate with Levin’s
       observations that the boundary between self and world in biology is fluid and configurable, e.g.
       during embryogenesis or in chimeric organisms 17 . Cadena’s idealism naturally accounts for such
       fluidity: since all selves are part of one whole, their boundaries are conceptual and can change with
       context.


     • Informational Ontology (Physics as Information): The God Equation implies that the physical
       world is information. Every electron, every field quantum, is like a bit or qubit in the Source’s cosmic
       computer. Space and time themselves may emerge from an informational structure (similar to digital
       physics proposals by physicists like Zuse or Fredkin). Cadena’s innovation is to integrate this with
       consciousness: the bits are also “thoughts” in the mind of the Source. Hence, reality is composed of
       informational thoughts. This yields a framework in which one can reinterpret physical phenomena
       as communication. For example, forces might be viewed as exchanges of information between
       particles (minds), and the laws of physics are akin to the grammar of the Source’s language. It’s a
       grand unification not only of forces (as physicists seek) but of the very categories of mind and
       matter.


In summary, Cadena’s The God Equation provides a metaphysical narrative that the universe is a purposeful,
conscious, informational whole. This narrative, while speculative, is crafted to be compatible with
scientific knowledge: it doesn’t reject evolution or physics, but rather embeds them in a larger context of
meaning. Where it goes beyond current science is in attributing agency and intent to the fundamental level
of reality. The rest of this literature review and subsequent analysis will examine how Dr. Michael Levin’s
empirical work – surprisingly and largely independently – has been uncovering signs of agency, memory,
and goal-directedness at the fundamental levels of biological organization. The striking parallels between
Cadena’s Source metaphysics and Levin’s findings in cells and tissues set the stage for a fertile synthesis.


2.2 Michael Levin’s Empirical Findings: Bioelectricity and Cognitive Biology

Michael Levin’s research can be situated in the broader field of regenerative biology and bioelectrical
pattern formation, but its implications extend into cognitive science and even philosophy of mind. Over




                                                      6
the past two decades, Levin and colleagues have explored how networks of cells use electrical signals
(voltage gradients and ion flows) to coordinate complex behaviors such as building anatomical structures,
healing injuries, and even forming novel living machines (e.g., xenobots). What makes Levin’s work
revolutionary is the interpretation: rather than viewing cells as mere biochemical machines executing
genetic programs, he views them as agents that communicate, compute, and make decisions towards
higher-level goals (like “build an organ here” or “regenerate a correct limb”). This agent-based view of cells
amounts to ascribing a primitive kind of intelligence or cognition to living tissue – an idea that was once
on the fringe (associated perhaps with 19th-century vitalism or 20th-century cybernetic speculation) but
now is supported by concrete experimental data.


Several key findings and concepts from Levin’s work are crucial for this discussion:


     • Bioelectric Signals as Morphogenetic “Information”: Levin has shown that cells in developing or
       regenerating organisms establish electric fields and voltage gradients that serve as instructive
       signals – effectively, a communication layer independent of biochemical pathways. For instance, in
       frog embryos, an early bioelectric “map” (sometimes visible as an electric face pattern) predicts the
       layout of the face before any genes for eye or jaw have been activated 18 19 . If this electrical
       pattern is disrupted or altered, the resulting anatomy changes. Levin calls this the bioelectric code –
       analogous to the genetic code but at the level of cell networks. It represents a kind of distributed
       memory or set-point of what the organism should look like. One experiment created a visual of a
       frog embryo’s electric face: it revealed localized regions of membrane voltage that correspond to
       where the eyes, nose, and mouth would form 20 19 . This suggests that the developing organism
       “knows” its goal shape in advance, encoded electrically. Such findings align with the idea that form is
       guided by information. The bioelectric pattern is essentially information that tells cells collectively,
       “this is the plan.” We might analogize it to a software blueprint that the cells (the hardware) execute.


     • Editing Anatomical Memory: Taking the bioelectric code concept further, Levin’s lab demonstrated
       the ability to edit this code and thereby alter morphology. A spectacular example involves planarian
       flatworms, which usually have one head and one tail. By manipulating certain ion channels to
       change the electrical connectivity among cells, Levin’s team induced some worms to regenerate with
       two heads (one at each end) 9 . Remarkably, these two-headed worms could be cut into pieces and
       each piece would regenerate again into a two-headed worm, indefinitely 21 22 . No change in DNA
       had occurred – genetically they were normal planaria – yet their body pattern “memory” had been
       rewritten in a lasting way. This indicates that the instructions for “how many heads” are stored in a
       living tissue medium (here, bioelectric circuits) and can be rewritten like data in a hard drive 2 22 .
       From a cognitive perspective, it’s as if the organism’s body has learned a new target morphology
       and will now aim to regenerate that shape. Levin often uses this example to argue that what we see
       in these worms is a primitive form of learning and memory at the tissue level – the worm’s body
       learned a new “goal state” (two-headedness) and remembers it without needing genomic changes
         23  24 . This blurs the line between developmental biology and memory systems: traditionally

       memory is in brains, but here memory is in somatic tissues.


     • Planarian Trainability and Decapitation Memory: Another striking finding relates to behavior and
       memory. Planaria can be trained in simple tasks (such as navigating a maze or avoiding a stimulus).
       Classic experiments in the 1960s (by McConnell) and more rigorous ones by Levin’s group showed
       that if you train a planarian worm, then cut off its head (which contains the brain), the worm will
       grow a new head and the memories can still be present 25 26 . In Levin’s 2013 paper, planaria

 regeneration 8 . This implies that the memory was not completely localized in the planarian brain;
 it was stored elsewhere in the body or in the collective network of cells. It underscores the concept of
 the body as a memory substrate 27 . The mechanisms are still under investigation – possibilities
 include RNA-based memory, bioelectric circuit imprinting, or epigenetic modifications – but the

 Philosophically, this finding resonates with the notion of a ubiquitous information field (Cadena’s

• Cognition All The Way Down – Scalable Minds: A unifying theme in Levin’s theoretical work is that
  cognition is scale-invariant in a sense, or present at multiple scales. He and his collaborators (e.g.,
  in the context of the Technological Approach to Mind Everywhere or TAME framework) propose that we
  should treat even unconventional systems as potential agents if they demonstrate problem-solving,
  memory, and goal-directed behavior 30 31 . He asks: “Where is the interface between the mental and
  the physical? When does the mental become the physical?” – and answers: “never… it is mental all the way
  down” 3 . This is a profound statement from a scientist: Levin is effectively endorsing a form of
  panpsychism or at least pan-cognitivism. He does not claim a rock has a mind in the human sense,
  but he suggests that the distinction between inanimate matter and mind is one of degree, not kind.
  For example, single cells show chemotaxis (moving toward nutrients) which can be seen as the most
  basic goal-directed behavior. Bacterial colonies solve problems like finding energy and even exhibit
  rudimentary memory (e.g., habituation to stimuli) 32 33 . Levin notes that even molecules might
  have proto-cognitive properties: he points out that gene regulatory networks can perform learning-
  like behaviors (six different kinds of learning were demonstrated in a simple network of DNA and
  proteins) 34 , and he speculates that even physics – for instance, the principle of least action in
  physics – could be interpreted teleologically (a system “chooses” an optimal path) 31 . While he
  frames this carefully as conjecture, it aligns with his overall view that competence and agency come
  in a continuum from low-level to high-level systems. He coined the term “cognitive light cone” to
  measure an agent’s intelligence by the spatio-temporal scale of its goals: an organism with a larger
  cognitive light cone can plan further ahead in time and across broader space 35 . Importantly, this
  framework doesn’t impose a binary cognitive vs non-cognitive distinction; rather everything from a
  thermostat (with a tiny light cone: one room, immediate time) to a human (many years and global
  concerns) to a hypothetical planetary mind (centuries, planet-wide) fits on the spectrum. This
  perspective is empirically motivated (by observing various living systems) but philosophically it
  provides a natural bridge to Cadena’s cosmology: if it’s “cognition all the way down” in Levin’s
  empirical purview, Cadena’s claim that the universe as a whole is cognitive (all the way up, as it were)
  becomes less extraordinary.


• Teleology in Biology (Teleonomy vs Teleology): Levin stands out among biologists for openly
  discussing purpose in living systems. He acknowledges the historical aversion to teleological
  language in biology (what he humorously calls “teleophobia” among scientists) 36 37 . But he argues
  that it’s now safe – and indeed necessary – to talk about goal-directedness because we have scientific
  frameworks (like cybernetics, control theory, and AI) that treat goals in mechanistic terms 38 39 . In
  his view, a thermostat’s functioning (maintaining a set temperature) is a simple form of teleology,

 and advanced organisms simply have more complex layers of such goal-seeking loops 40 41 . Levin
 uses teleonomy to refer to apparent purposefulness that arises from natural processes, but he
 emphasizes that this is not a trick of perspective alone – there is a real sense in which biological
 systems have goals and try to achieve them, even if those goals are evolutionarily programmed or
 arose by selection 42 43 . For example, a regenerating limb “knows” when to stop growing because
 it has a built-in target morphology 44 1 . Experiments with grafting showed that if an axolotl (a
 regenerative salamander) tail is grafted to the flank, it will remodel into a limb – cells didn’t just keep
 a tail structure, they adjusted to what should be there in that position 45 46 . This implies a holistic
 awareness of the correct anatomical context. Levin describes this using intentional language: the
 cells “realize” there is an error (a tail where a leg should be) and collectively work to fix it 46 47 . All
 of this suggests that teleological explanations (in terms of ends and goals) have descriptive and
 predictive power in biology. Levin’s stance rehabilitates teleology by grounding it in empiricism: for
 him, teleology is not mysticism but an observable property of complex systems that contain internal
 feedback loops striving towards states.


• Collective Intelligence and Scale: Levin frequently emphasizes that intelligence is collective. He
  quips that all intelligence we know of is collective intelligence 48 49 – a single neuron isn’t smart,
  but a brain of billions is; a single cell isn’t human-level smart, but a body of trillions of cells is;
  conversely, even an individual human might be seen as a collective of smaller agents (cells) and also
  as part of larger socio-technological minds. This fractal view of intelligence means there is no
  privileged scale at which cognition happens. It resonates with Cadena’s view that individual minds
  are parts of a bigger mind. Levin gives the example of Rene Descartes’s search for the seat of soul
  in the pineal gland: Descartes favored it because it’s singular, but if he had a microscope he’d see it’s
  made of many cells, thus undermining a singular self inside 50 51 . Levin’s point is that there is no
  “indivisible” smallest self; everything is made of parts, down to particles (and physics even shows
  particles may be excitations of fields, etc.) 52 . Therefore, individuality is somewhat an arbitrary
  demarcation – a point also made by Cadena philosophically. Levin’s concept of scaling of the
  cognitive light cone 35 and his focus on how parts integrate into wholes directly address the
  question of how larger selves (like a human mind) emerge from smaller ones (cells). He posits that
  bioelectricity is one key mechanism enabling this integration: it’s a “cognitive glue” that binds cells
  into a network, allowing them to share information and pursue a larger goal together 53 54 . In his
  words, bioelectric networks let “individual competent cells merge into larger emergent individuals”
  by coordinating their perspectives 53 . This is essentially a theory of how a higher-level self (the
  multicellular organism) forms and maintains coherence – an empirical counterpart to Cadena’s
  philosophical question of how one Source subdivides into many and yet can act as one.


• Facilitating Ingression of Intelligence: One particularly intriguing idea Levin has floated in
  interviews (not formal papers yet) is that when we create new forms of life or AI, we are not
  manufacturing intelligence from scratch, but rather enabling pre-existing intelligence to enter a new
  embodiment 16 . He states: “I don’t believe we make intelligence… I think we facilitate the ingression of
  intelligence when we make embodiments like embryos and [AIs]” 16 . This sounds almost mystical – as if
  intelligence exists in some platonic realm (or pervades the universe) and new physical forms just tap
  into it. However, given Levin’s otherwise naturalistic approach, one can interpret this in a non-
  dualistic way: because his framework sees intelligence as a continuum in nature, any time we
  arrange matter in a complex, feedback-rich system (like a brain or a sophisticated AI network), we
  are opening a channel for the ambient cognitive potential of the universe to flow into that
  structure. This is remarkably compatible with Cadena’s Source idea: Cadena would say the Source’s

       universal mind is what flows or ingresses into any suitable “vessel”. Levin’s remark captures the
       intuition that mind is not created by us; it is attracted or guided into form. While Levin might
       mean it metaphorically (pointing out that even human-designed AI will likely tap into emergent
       dynamics we don’t program, hence we “conjure” rather than program minds 55 ), it nevertheless
       aligns with an idealist view where mind is primary and matter secondary.


To summarize Levin’s contributions: he provides a scientifically grounded perspective that life is
fundamentally about information processing and goal-directed organization. Biological phenomena
traditionally explained in mechanistic terms can alternatively be explained in cognitive terms (the body
“wants” to achieve a particular form; cells “communicate” and “decide” their fates; evolution finds
“strategies” as if solving problems). Levin’s work gives these assertions concrete meaning: the bioelectric
code and experiments in rewriting it show that we can pinpoint the informational medium and even
manipulate it, treating the organism as a reprogrammable intelligent system 56 57 . This not only
revolutionizes biology (imagine regenerative medicine that convinces tissues to regrow limbs by telling
them the right bioelectric story 14 58 ), but it has philosophical weight: it suggests that agency and
intentionality are natural properties of organized matter.


In the context of this dissertation, Levin’s empirical findings will serve as the bridge points where Cadena’s
metaphysical claims can connect to observable reality. Each of the thematic areas – consciousness, purpose,
evolution, memory, selfhood, informational nature – finds echoes between Cadena and Levin. The literature
reviewed here shows that we have precedent and language to discuss such interdisciplinary integration:
philosophers of biology like Terence Deacon (whom Levin has dialogued with) speak of “incomplete nature”
and emergent teleology 59 43 , cognitive scientists like John Vervaeke (with whom Levin collaborated)
explore concepts of distributed cognition and relevance realization, and even mainstream neuroscience is
rethinking metaphors (hence the panel “Your Brain is not a Computer” involving Levin) 60 61 . Thus, our
synthesis is not occurring in a vacuum; it is a continuation of a trend to dissolve old boundaries – between
mind and matter, between philosophy and science, between individual and collective – in favor of a more
holistic, information-centric worldview.

Methodology
Bridging a metaphysical thesis with empirical science requires a careful interdisciplinary methodology. This
dissertation employs a conceptual synthesis approach underpinned by comparative analysis of texts
(philosophical arguments and scientific reports), qualitative content analysis of interview transcripts, and
elements of theoretical modeling. The goal is not to perform a new laboratory experiment or derive a
formal equation, but rather to develop a unified conceptual framework that is coherent, philosophically
sound, and consistent with known empirical data. Below, I detail the methodological steps and principles
that guided this work:


     • Comparative Thematic Analysis: A set of core themes was identified (as specified by the
       dissertation brief): consciousness, purpose, evolution, memory, selfhood, and informational ontology.
       These themes serve as lenses through which both Cadena’s and Levin’s works were examined. For
       each theme, key propositions from Cadena’s The God Equation were extracted (drawing from the text



                                                     10
 and summarizing its arguments), and key findings or assertions from Levin’s research and interviews
 were collated. This was akin to creating two columns – one for Cadena, one for Levin – under each
 theme and populating them with summarized content and direct quotes. The methodology here
 resembles a qualitative coding process: the transcripts of Levin’s interviews (provided in user files)
 were coded for instances where these themes appear (e.g., Levin speaking about “consciousness” or
 “goals” or “memory”). The same was done, conceptually, for Cadena’s writing. This thematic mapping
 ensured that our integration addresses each major dimension in a structured way and that we do
 justice to both sources on their own terms before blending them.


• Conceptual Mapping (Figure/Table Construction): Based on the comparative analysis above, a
  conceptual map was constructed to visually and schematically represent the relationships between
  Cadena’s metaphysical concepts and Levin’s empirical concepts. The conceptual map (presented in
  Chapter 4) takes the form of a detailed table, wherein each row corresponds to one of the major
  themes and columns juxtapose Cadena’s perspective, Levin’s perspective, and the synthesized
  unified perspective. The process of building this map is methodological: it forced an explicit
  identification of parallels and discrepancies. For instance, in the “Consciousness” row, Cadena’s
  column notes “Consciousness as universal, fundamental (the Source’s nature)” and Levin’s column
  notes “Cognition all the way down; cells and tissues have proto-consciousness 3 .” The act of
  placing these side by side and articulating a synthesis (e.g., “Unified view: Consciousness is a multi-
  scale phenomenon with the Source as ultimate integrator, and cells as local minds”) is a
  methodological step of theory synthesis. It ensures a systematic coverage of each aspect and
  becomes a heuristic device to check consistency: a unified framework should not contradict known
  evidence in either column, and where evidence is lacking on one side (e.g., Cadena’s claims might be
  more speculative on a given theme), the framework should make this clear and potentially suggest
  how Levin’s science could fill the gap.


• Interdisciplinary Literature Contextualization: While the focus is on Cadena and Levin, the
  methodology included reviewing related literature to ground the discussion academically. This
  means situating Cadena’s ideas among other metaphysical theories (e.g., citing Bernardo Kastrup for
  analytic idealism, or Alfred North Whitehead for process philosophy, etc.) and situating Levin’s ideas
  among other scientific paradigms (e.g., mentioning the field of cybernetics, systems biology, or the
  enactive approach in cognitive science). This literature context served two purposes: (1) to avoid
  reinventing concepts that already exist (for example, when synthesizing, recognizing that the unified
  framework has precedents like dual-aspect monism or panpsychism, which can be acknowledged and
  used to bolster credibility), and (2) to identify potential criticisms and address them. For example,
  one might ask: is attributing “agency” to cells just anthropomorphism? Methodologically, I would
  answer by pointing to how control theory in engineering attributes “goals” to machines without
  mystification, and how Levin explicitly uses that analogy 40 41 . Thus, part of the method is
  argumentative: pre-empting and responding to likely objections by drawing on existing scholarly
  discourse.


• Use of Interview Transcripts as Data: A significant methodological component is the use of
  primary source transcripts from Michael Levin’s interviews and lectures (provided by the user).
  These transcripts are treated as qualitative data, capturing Levin’s viewpoints in his own words. They
  are a rich resource because they often contain more speculative and philosophical remarks by Levin
  that may not appear in peer-reviewed papers, and these are precisely the remarks that interface well
  with Cadena’s metaphysics. Each relevant excerpt was carefully vetted to ensure it is understood in




                                                11
 context (the transcripts often have moderators or other speakers, so I ensured the quotes used are
 indeed Levin’s and reflect his stance, not just a question or a hypothetical). These excerpts are cited
 in the text in the special format (as per the guidelines) to maintain a clear link to the source. In an
 appendix (Appendix A), I compile the most salient quotes along with Harvard-style citations and brief
 annotations. This serves a dual purpose: to maintain transparency about the evidence basis (so that
 claims about Levin’s views are directly backed by his own statements) and to provide a resource that
 cross-references the unified framework’s claims with source material. The transcripts function
 similarly to interview data in a qualitative research project: I performed a content analysis (coding for
 themes, as mentioned) and am now using representative quotations as evidence for the analysis.


• Philosophical Argumentation and Synthesis: The act of synthesis goes beyond listing parallels – it
  involves philosophical argumentation to weave a coherent worldview. Methodologically, this
  meant constructing arguments for why the unified framework is plausible and valuable. For
  instance, to argue that “bioelectricity is the physical mechanism through which Source intelligence
  guides organisms,” I proceed by logical steps: (a) show that bioelectricity in Levin’s work carries
  instructive information (evidence: experiments producing specific outcomes via electrical tweaks) 2
    56 ; (b) invoke Cadena’s claim that mind/information underlies matter, implying that if the Source

  were to influence biology, an information-rich channel like bioelectricity is a likely candidate (this is a
  theoretical inference, not proof); (c) propose that therefore bioelectric fields could be a substrate for
  downward causation (mind influencing matter) in the unified framework. This kind of argument
  requires careful handling of epistemic status – it’s not “proven” that cosmic consciousness acts
  through bioelectricity, but it’s a consistent hypothesis that explains both Cadena’s and Levin’s
  content. The methodology here is akin to inference to the best explanation: we look at surprising
  empirical facts (like a cell group “knowing” to stop growth at the right time, or a tumor being
  normalized by an electric tweak 15 ) and see if the metaphysical framework provides a more
  comprehensive explanation than a reductionist approach.


• First-Person Perspective (Reflexive Methodology): As Cadena, the author of The God Equation, I
  occasionally employ a reflexive, first-person approach. In academic practice, especially in philosophy,
  it is acceptable to articulate one’s position in the first person for clarity (“I argue that…”, “In my
  view…”). Here, doing so also serves to acknowledge authorial perspective. It reminds the reader
  that the unified framework is a novel contribution by Cadena, synthesizing others’ ideas but also
  injecting my own creative reasoning. This reflexive element ensures transparency about potential
  biases: obviously, as the proponent of Source metaphysics, I am motivated to see support for it in
  biology – and acknowledging that helps maintain intellectual honesty. Throughout the work,
  however, I strive to maintain a formal tone and ground statements in either evidence or clearly
  stated logical inference, to adhere to rigorous academic standards expected at Harvard.


• Scope Delimitation: Methodologically, it’s important to delimit what this synthesis will and will not
  do. It does not, for instance, propose new laboratory experiments (though in the Conclusion I might
  suggest future empirical tests as implications). It also does not try to force a literal identity of terms
  (I recognize, for example, that Levin’s use of “cognition” may not mean subjective experience, and
  Cadena’s “consciousness” has cosmic breadth; part of the method is then to define terms carefully in
  the synthesis to avoid equivocation – e.g., distinguishing levels of mind). The focus is on conceptual
  unification and philosophical interpretation, not on mathematically formulating the “God
  Equation” (which remains a metaphor here). That said, a methodological openness is maintained: if
  some aspects of Cadena’s thesis found no support or even contradictions in Levin’s data, the unified




                                                  12
       framework would note that tension rather than sweep it under the rug. For example, if Levin had
       evidence that some processes are fundamentally random or without purpose, we would have to
       accommodate that (in truth, Levin acknowledges randomness in evolution but argues we can do
       better via engineering).


      • Academic Tone and Citation Style: Consistent with Harvard academic expectations, all significant
        claims or direct uses of sources are cited. The methodology includes using Harvard style for final
        references and a bracketed citation format in text for clarity on source provenance 3 . This
        meticulous citation practice is a methodological choice to bolster credibility: when merging
        disciplines, skeptics from each side can be appeased if they see proper references. A philosopher will
        see that I haven’t misrepresented what the science says (because I cite it), and a biologist will see I
        engage with philosophical sources in context. The combination of in-text bracket citations and a
        formal bibliography meets the user’s requirements and ensures traceability.


In conclusion, the methodology is inherently interdisciplinary and integrative. It blends qualitative
analysis (of texts and transcripts), comparative charting, and philosophical argumentation. The reliability of
the synthesis is reinforced by the extensive use of direct sources, while its creativity comes from the
interpretive leaps between those sources. This approach is appropriate for a dissertation in Interdisciplinary
Studies: it does not confine itself to one field’s methods but borrows and blends methods to fit the hybrid
nature of the inquiry. The next chapter (Chapter 4) will present the Levin–Cadena Unified Framework
itself, effectively the outcome of this methodological process, structured around the thematic comparisons
and featuring the conceptual map (table) as a centerpiece for discussion.


Introducing the Levin–Cadena Unified Framework
Chapter 4: The Levin–Cadena Unified Framework


In this chapter, the dissertation’s core contribution is presented: the Levin–Cadena Unified Framework.
This framework is an integrated model that reconciles and synthesizes Cadena’s Source-based metaphysics
with Levin’s empirical findings on bioelectricity and multi-scale cognition. It posits a unified reality in which
consciousness (mind) and morphology (matter) are deeply intertwined through informational
structures. The framework can be summarized as follows:


      • Ontological Unification: The universe is fundamentally composed of informational consciousness
        (the Source), and physical organisms are embodiments of that information, operating as cognitive
        agents at various scales. There is no absolute divide between mind and matter; rather, all matter
        contains mind-like properties (as Levin’s continuum suggests) 3 , and all mind expresses through
        lawful information patterns (as Cadena’s metaphysics suggests).


      • Bioelectricity as Interface: Bioelectric fields and signals in living systems act as the interface
        between higher-order consciousness (the Source or universal mind) and the physical body. In
        essence, bioelectricity is the mechanism by which information (and thus intention or “will”) is
        translated into physiological outcomes. This provides a concrete channel for what metaphysics
        might term “downward causation”: the Source influencing material outcomes via structured
        information fields.




                                                       13
• Multi-Scale Cognition and Source Continuum: Intelligence and agency are present at all levels –
  from the Source itself (the sum total of all intelligence) down to cells and molecules (which possess
  rudimentary agency). The Levin–Cadena framework sees these not as separate intelligences, but as a
  nested hierarchy or continuum of mind. Each level (molecule, cell, organ, organism, ecosystem,
  planet, etc.) is a “holon” with a degree of autonomy and awareness, but also part of larger holons,
  ultimately part of the Source. This echoes Levin’s “nested society of agents” view 62 63 enriched by
  Cadena’s view that the nesting goes all the way up to a singular cosmic agent.


• Teleology and Goal-Directed Evolution: The unified framework holds that purpose is real at every
  level. Cells have goals (e.g., wound healing, homeostasis), organisms have goals (survival,
  flourishing), and the cosmos itself has a directional tendency (toward greater complexity, awareness,
  or what Cadena calls self-realization of the Source). This means evolution is not random in an
  absolute sense; it is informed by an informational attractor – life tends toward forms that allow
  the Source to more fully manifest its possibilities (more complex, conscious forms). Levin’s empirical
  observation that life sometimes behaves as if working toward specific outcomes (morphological set-
  points, regenerative goals) 44 6 is seen as a microcosm of the cosmic teleology Cadena proposes.
  In effect, biological teleology is a local instance of cosmic teleology.


• Memory and Morphogenesis: Memory is understood on multiple scales: organisms have memory
  (even without brains, as planaria show 8 ), and the universe has memory (the persistence of
  information, perhaps akin to physical conservation laws or even reincarnational memory in spiritual
  terms). The framework suggests that morphogenetic fields – such as Levin’s bioelectric maps –
  are stores of memory about form, potentially connected to a broader informational field that
  carries past “learnings” of evolution. For instance, the reason a salamander’s cells “know” how to
  regrow a limb might be because that information is encoded in a field that transcends the individual
  instance (this is speculative, but it provides a metaphysical explanation for why bioelectric signals
  carry so much instructive power: they could be tapping into a field of collective biological memory,
  conceptually related to Cadena’s Source memory). In short, biological form is guided by something
  akin to a memory of the ideal form, aligning with Cadena’s view of Platonic ideals stored in the
  Source’s mind.


• Selfhood and Boundary Fluidity: The framework postulates that individual selfhood is a scale-
  relative, dynamic boundary within the unified field of mind. Levin’s work shows that the boundary
  of what constitutes a coherent self can shift – for example, during embryogenesis multiple cells form
  one self (the embryo), in chimeras two embryos might merge, in cancer a part of the cellular
  collective breaks away from the unified goal 17 . Cadena’s metaphysics concurs that selves are not
  absolute partitions but partitions of convenience in one mind. Thus the unified framework predicts
  phenomena like those Levin describes – e.g., a tumor is essentially a dissociated sub-agency that has
  lost integration with the whole (Levin analogizes tumors to “dissociative identity disorder” at the
  cellular collective level 15 ). The Source can be thought of as the ultimate unified self that in healthy
  systems lends coherence, whereas pathology (in body or psyche) might be viewed as disruptions in
  the informational connectivity to the Source (manifesting as sub-units pursuing rogue goals).


• Epistemological Implication: If this framework is correct, then to truly understand any complex
  system (be it an embryo or an ecosystem), one must account for both material causes and
  informational/teleological causes. Practically, this means expanding scientific methodology to
  detect and interpret informational signals (like bioelectric patterns, but perhaps also other fields) as

      computation or healing as a search process toward an anatomical goal. It also suggests new
      experiments – for instance, testing whether altering bioelectric patterns in one organism could
      “imprint” information in another, hinting at an informational field connection beyond local
      communication (a hypothesis that, if observed, would be direct evidence of a Source field effect).
      While such experiments border on what some might call parapsychology, the unified framework

4.1 Consciousness: Universal Mind and Cellular Minds

At the foundation of the unified framework is a redefinition of consciousness: rather than a peculiar
emergent property of certain neural networks, consciousness is understood as an inherent aspect of the
informational universe, present (in differing degrees or forms) at all levels. Cadena’s position that the
cosmos is a universal consciousness (the Source) finds a natural complement in Levin’s experimental stance
that even cells and tissues have rudimentary cognition. In the unified framework, these two are not
contradictory but continuous. Levin’s assertion that “it’s mental all the way down… from high level minds... all
the way down to molecules” 3 is almost a direct empirical endorsement of Cadena’s cosmopsychism. It
suggests that if we were to go “all the way up” in scale beyond human minds, we might likewise find that it’s
mental all the way up – i.e., reality as a whole is mental (which is precisely Cadena’s starting point).


By merging these views, the framework depicts a nested hierarchy of consciousness. Consider an
analogy: a Russian doll structure of minds within minds. A single human cell has a very minimal awareness



                                                      22
(perhaps just the ability to sense its environment and act accordingly – what neuroscientist E. Kandel called
the “perception-action” loop of even single-celled organisms). A collection of cells (say a tissue or organ) can
have a more complex collective awareness – for example, a pancreas regulating blood sugar “knows” the
glucose level and responds appropriately, a goal-directed action, though it has no brain. An entire human
has the unified consciousness we recognize as our mind, which arises from the coordination of billions of
cells. Scaling up, a community or society could be seen as having a group mind (in weak forms this is just
culture, in stronger speculative forms, perhaps a real collective consciousness). Cadena’s Source would be
the limit of this progression: the all-inclusive mind containing and constituting everything.


In this nested model, individual consciousness is like a subsection of a larger consciousness, separated
not by absolute barriers but by relative informational isolation. Levin’s description of humans as
“dissociative personas” of a universal mind 69 82 – a scenario he entertains when discussing advanced
intelligence realizing unity – aligns perfectly. He muses that advanced intelligences might regard separate
beings as “dissociative alters of a universal mind” 69 . The unified framework simply takes that proposition
earnestly: indeed, we are alters of the Source, partially dissociated to have individual experiences. This
provides a philosophical justification for why multiple perspectives (multiple selves) exist at once despite an
underlying unity: it is the only way for the Source to experience multiplicity and relationships.


An important implication of this view is that consciousness is not created by brains, but rather collected
or funneled by them. This resonates with Levin’s remark on “ingression of intelligence” 16 . In the unified
framework, a brain (or any complex information-processing structure, biological or artificial) is like a radio
receiver tuning into a certain band of the Source’s consciousness. When the brain is assembled and active, it
localizes a portion of the universal mind – resulting in an individual consciousness with specific content. This
is akin to the transmission hypothesis of mind (proposed historically by figures like William James and
Bergson), which Cadena’s metaphysics naturally supports: the brain does not produce consciousness any
more than a radio produces music; rather, the brain tunes into and shapes a consciousness that was already
“in the air” (the Source field). Levin’s perspective provides a tangible mechanism: bioelectric patterns and
neural networks could be the tuning circuitry that connects the individual to the wider field. When he
says we “facilitate ingression of intelligence” by creating embryos or AIs 16 , the picture is of minds pre-
existing and flowing in when conditions permit – which is exactly what the Source cosmology would predict.


By recognizing consciousness at low levels, the framework also changes how we interpret certain scientific
results. For instance, when bacteria solve problems like optimizing nutrient uptake, or slime molds solve
mazes, these are often described in biology as interesting but ultimately mechanistic feats. The unified
framework would say: No, these organisms have genuine (if minimal) subjective aims and awareness. Not
human-like, of course, but real in their context. Levin’s comfort with terms like “cognitive” for non-neural
systems 67 68 backs this. One might ask: how can a molecule be conscious? The answer lies in
recognizing degrees of freedom: a molecule might have an extremely faint spark of protoconsciousness –
perhaps just a tendency or bias – far from reflective thought, but still on the spectrum. In physics, some
interpretations of quantum mechanics even allow for a form of proto-awareness choosing outcomes
(however, the framework doesn’t hinge on quantum mind ideas, it simply doesn’t exclude them).


In summary, under the unified framework, consciousness is a field (or continuum) and individual
conscious entities are loci within that field. Levin’s cells and tissues exemplify that this field extends into
the living world deeply; Cadena’s Source hypothesis extends it to encompass all reality. The coherence of
these ideas is exemplified by how easily Levin can discuss panpsychist notions: he does not find it
“controversial” that all intelligence is collective and made of parts 48 52 . This is a rejection of Cartesian

as the “combination problem” (how do individual minds combine into a larger mind?). Here, individual
minds don’t so much combine as they were never truly separate; they are overlapping regions in the single
mind, with bioelectric and other physical structures acting as semi-permeable boundaries that let
information (hence awareness) pool in certain configurations.


4.2 Purpose: Teleology Revisited from Cells to Cosmos

The unified framework unabashedly embraces teleology, the idea that processes are driven by goals or
ends. This is a bold stance, given that modern science has, for centuries, expunged formal and final causes
in favor of efficient causes. Yet both Cadena and Levin independently resurrect teleological thinking, each in
their own domain – Cadena at the cosmic level (the universe has purpose), Levin at the biological level
(organisms have built-in goal states). By bringing these together, we propose that the teleology observed
in living systems is a local manifestation of a universal teleology.


At the cosmic level, Cadena’s Source is not a static container of being; it is dynamic and oriented. Why would
the Source give rise to a universe at all? The answer implied by The God Equation is that the Source has a
kind of intentionality: to explore all possible experiences, to increase the richness of reality, or
metaphorically, to “know itself.” This is reminiscent of spiritual philosophies (for example, in Vedanta,
Brahman creates the world to experience itself) but Cadena couches it in information terms. The Source
runs the God Equation to maximize informational entropy (diversity) but within a structure that leads to
complexity, not chaos. In other words, the Source’s telos (ultimate goal) might be the maximization of
meaningful complexity – more conscious beings, deeper knowledge, more integrated forms. If we look at
the 13.8 billion year history of the universe, we do see a trend from simplicity (a near-uniform plasma) to
complexity (atoms, molecules, stars, life, minds). Standard science explains this trend through selection and
the flow of energy (Prigogine’s far-from-equilibrium thermodynamics, etc.), but one can ask: why do these
laws permit increasing complexity? The unified framework answers: because the laws themselves are the
expression of the Source’s teleology. The second law of thermodynamics (tendency to increasing entropy)
coexists with local decreases in entropy (life, galaxies) because overall the information in the universe is
increasing – and information is linked to entropy but not identical; structured information can increase even
as entropy increases. Life is a negentropy phenomenon but as Schrödinger said, it feeds on entropy to
create order. This might be exactly how the Source implements teleology physically: through processes that
locally fight entropy and create structure.

4.3 Evolution: From Random Variation to Informational Evolution

The Darwinian paradigm of evolution by natural selection is often taken to imply purposelessness –
organisms change due to random mutations and those that happen to be advantageous get preserved.
However, there have always been undercurrents of thought suggesting evolution has directions or trends
(e.g., increasing complexity, or convergence to similar solutions as if certain forms are “attractors”).
Cadena’s metaphysics explicitly claims that evolution (cosmic and biological) is guided by the Source’s aims,
whereas Levin’s work – while not directly about evolution of species – provides hints that evolution has
endowed organisms with remarkable problem-solving capacities (which suggests evolution itself “solved
problems” to get here).


The unified framework acknowledges the mechanism of mutation and selection but posits an additional
factor: informational guidance. This might manifest as what some theorists call “directed
mutation” (controversial evidence that some organisms mutate more in useful ways under stress), or simply
as the initial setup of physical constants favoring rich chemistry. Levin notes that evolution did not design
things top-down, but it provided tools like electrical signaling that are so versatile they let organisms
explore many possibilities without new genes 85 12 . For instance, the same genome in a frog can produce
a normal tadpole or, if cells are reconfigured, a “xenobot” – a novel living machine that crawls around, which
Levin’s team created from frog cells. That the cells could assemble into something functional and not just a
random lump suggests that evolutionary potential is latent – cells have intelligences and plasticity allowing
adaptation to novel configurations. In our framework, this can be seen as life’s built-in tendency to seek
viable forms (the Source’s evolutionary creativity emerging). Xenobots self-organized to move in
coordinated ways, as if even when we scramble the context, they try to achieve some coherent behavior
rather than act randomly.


Cadena would likely interpret that as the Source’s influence: cells know how to be useful even outside their
normal body plan because they partake in a general intelligence of living matter. Indeed, Levin often
emphasizes that evolution exploits the intelligence of cells: rather than micromanage every detail by genes,
evolution sets up cell systems that can figure things out (solve problems) on the fly 86 6 . He calls this
“swarm cognition” or collective intelligence. The unified framework adds: why would evolution do that?
Because at the informational level, it’s more efficient to delegate to intelligent sub-agents. This starts to
sound like attributing wisdom to evolution – which is not far from saying the Source imbued evolution with
a drive to create increasingly intelligent, adaptable systems.


Another supportive observation is the prevalence of convergent evolution – independent lineages arriving
at very similar solutions (eyes, wings, etc.). Some biologists (like Simon Conway Morris) argue convergence
implies some attractor states in the fitness landscape, possibly pointing to deeper laws. Under our
framework, those attractors could be the Source’s “preferred” structures – forms that allow for certain
experiences or functions. Eyes, for example, vastly expand the informational intake for an organism, which
fits the Source’s goal of richer experience. So multiple lineages got eyes because eyes were an inherent

possibility that the Source’s informational field biased life toward (through selection making it highly
favorable wherever it could happen).

But focusing on empirical: one of Levin’s noted points is how evolution re-uses patterns across scales. The

hardware” (ion-based signaling) in single cells long before large brains – a hint that even from the start, life

Source’s aim was eventual intelligent life, laying down early the building blocks of nervous systems (even in

touches on that: he works on technologies to direct evolution (like creating new life forms, or guiding limb

aspects of reality by tapping into the Source’s information field (this drifts into what some might call “mind

see real, testable instances of field memory.


Lastly, consider that physics has the unresolved issue of information loss in black holes – currently it’s

reconstruct a previous brain state – but if the information of that state is also in the Source’s ledger, perhaps
that’s why memories can sometimes be startlingly detailed or why under hypnosis or unusual states people
recall things they didn’t think they encoded. Again, speculative, but the key is the framework does not treat
memory as only a neural quirk; it’s a fundamental phenomenon of an informational universe.

philosophy and neuroscience. The unified framework contends that the self is an emergent, dynamic
composite whose boundaries are defined by information flow, not by absolute dividing lines. This stance

Levin’s disagreement with Kastrup on compositionality 17 provides a crisp entry point: Kastrup (another
idealist) says a true individual must start individual; Levin says biology shows composition – multiple smaller
units coming together can form a new unified self (like the brain’s billions of neurons yielding a unified
mind). Cadena’s perspective is closer to Levin here: all individual selves are derived from the Source (so not
separate at origin) and can merge or split. For example, certain life forms merge (many unicellular fuse into
a multicellular colony that acts like one, or during embryogenesis originally separate cells become a single
organism’s body). There are also weird cases like organisms that can separate (some jellyfish that split into
two clones – was one self then became two). These biological realities underscore that the concept of “a
self” is somewhat flexible. The unified framework accounts for this by positing that consciousness (the

communication, a higher self “appears” because the Source’s mind now has a larger integrated subsystem
to operate through (like a multi-core processor forming one computing system). When a unified system
splits into two that no longer communicate, the consciousness effectively splits into two separate streams
(dissociation in psychological terms, or physical fission in organisms). Thus selves can emerge, dissolve,
merge, and split depending on connectivity.


We see this fluidity in phenomena Levin cites: in metamorphosis, a caterpillar’s body is radically reorganized
into a butterfly; some parts of its brain persist (and memories can persist through metamorphosis), but
effectively it’s a new instantiation of an organism. In cancer, as earlier mentioned, part of the cellular
collective stops listening and goes “rogue”, like a split personality in the body 15 . In colonial organisms, like
coral reefs or Portuguese man-o-war (a siphonophore which is many zooids functioning as one),
individuality is blurred. Our gut microbiome complicates “us” as well – are the microbes part of our self or
not? They’re other organisms, but they crucially affect our mind (gut-brain axis). The unified framework
would say the boundary of self can be drawn narrowly (just the human cells) or broadly (human +
symbionts + perhaps even tools we use intimately). Think of extended mind thesis in philosophy: our phone
or notebook becomes part of our cognition (memory storage). Thus, self is not fixed; it’s contextual and
functional.


Cadena’s monism implies that ultimately, there is just the Source Self. So why do we feel like distinct selves?
Because information is partitioned. My brain has access to some information that yours doesn’t and vice
versa, hence we have private experiences. The Source’s knowledge is effectively siloed into many channels
(like a large parallel computer running many processes that don’t fully share data). But sometimes, those
silos can connect (empathy, telepathy if it exists, collective effervescence in groups, etc.). In extreme cases,
mystics report feeling “one with everything” – interpreted in our framework as temporarily dissolving the
partitions and experiencing a taste of the Source’s unified perspective (maybe via quieting the brain’s
default mode network as some neuroscience studies of meditation show; the brain stops reinforcing the
ego boundary and consciousness seems to expand).


One might worry that if self is fluid, how is personal identity maintained at all? The answer is that stable
informational structures (like a fairly fixed network of neurons) provide continuity, and memory ensures we
link our present to our past, thus maintaining identity. Yet even in one lifetime, our self-concept can change
drastically (consider someone brainwashed or who has a drastic personality change after an injury – is he
the same person?). Legally and practically we often treat them as changed in some fundamental way. The

In Levin’s lexicon, the “cognitive light cone” and “scale of an agent” are ways to quantify a self’s scope 35 . A

Another biological angle: when cells combine into an organism, they must surrender some individuality

Thus, the unified framework suggests that selfhood is a spectrum and a process, not an all-or-nothing

minds are components.


One concrete implication: this view provides a philosophical basis for ethical consideration of collective

cognition (like studying how communities think and solve problems as one unit). Levin’s work implies such
multi-scale cognition is real (e.g., a slime mold network solves a maze as if one mind, though it’s many cells).


To conclude on selfhood: the Levin–Cadena framework demotes the “solidity” of the individual self, viewing

but permeable. The only truly solid self is the totality, Source, which is ever-present. This does not diminish
the value of individuals (each is a unique locus of experience which is the Source exploring itself), but it
frames death as not the absolute end (because the information of that life remains in Source’s memory and
their consciousness, at core a part of Source, isn’t destroyed but reintegrated). Likewise, birth is not creation
ex nihilo of a soul, but partitioning off of Source consciousness into a new vessel. In this way, the age-old
philosophical puzzle of how one gets souls aligning with bodies is resolved: there is only one “soul”, portions
of which are associated with each body by the channeling of information.

Levin’s success in controlling biology by addressing the information level (e.g., using light to trigger ion

Thus, life is a kind of biological computer. But not a trivial Turing machine, rather a goal-oriented self-

when electrons and other particles combine, the pattern of information becomes richer (atoms with orbitals
are information structures too). Chemistry is information processing (molecules have shapes that carry info
about what fits where). Biology just lifts that to a higher order (DNA is literally a digital code). And brains are
yet higher, encoding things like memories in synaptic weights.


Now, if at each jump in complexity we see information patterns carrying more abstract content (like a brain

In this chapter, we step back from the detailed comparison of Cadena’s and Levin’s ideas and consider the

5.1 A New Bridge Between Metaphysics and Science

Historically, metaphysics and empirical science have had a fraught relationship. Metaphysics ventures
grand ontological claims (e.g., “reality is fundamentally mental” or “purpose pervades existence”) that often
cannot be directly tested, whereas science prides itself on testability and often confines itself to narrower
questions (“how does this mechanism work?”). The Levin–Cadena framework is an attempt at a
convergence: it brings metaphysical claims into contact with empirical evidence, to the benefit of both.
Cadena’s idealist metaphysics gains concreteness and plausibility by finding support in observable
phenomena (such as cellular cognition and morphogenetic fields), and Levin’s empirical discoveries gain
depth of interpretation by being placed in a cosmological context that explains why biological systems
behave as they do (not just how).


In philosophical terms, this approach is a form of inference to the best explanation or what philosopher
Nicholas Rescher calls metaphysical synthesis: using scientific findings as clues to metaphysical truths. It
aligns with a current in philosophy of mind known as panpsychism or cosmopsychism that has re-
emerged in analytic philosophy (e.g., Galen Strawson, Philip Goff, Bernardo Kastrup) to address the “hard
problem” of consciousness by positing that consciousness is fundamental. Our framework is a specific
flavor of cosmopsychism (cosmic consciousness – the Source – is primary) but with an added structure: it
delineates how that cosmic consciousness manifests in graded form in living systems via information
patterns (a detail many philosophical treatments lack). Thus, it provides a mechanistic account for an
idealist ontology – something often missing in pure metaphysics. By pointing to bioelectricity, feedback
loops, and computational principles, we sketch a route for the “downward causation” from Source to matter:
it’s not magic, it’s mediated by information fields which science can in principle measure (indeed, measuring
bioelectric fields is part of Levin’s lab work).


One might ask: is this framework essentially a modern form of Spinoza’s dual-aspect monism (where the
one substance has mental and physical attributes)? In some sense yes – we say the one substance (Source/
information) has the mental aspect (conscious experience) and the physical aspect (informational structure
visible as matter/energy). However, we go further by saying the mental aspect is not uniform; it’s organized
in a multiscale way. This is more akin to Whitehead’s process philosophy, where reality is made of
“occasions of experience” and larger occasions are built from smaller ones. Our cells having experience and
contributing to ours is very Whiteheadian. The added value of Levin’s empirical angle is that we can identify
actual processes corresponding to Whitehead’s abstractions (e.g., a cell’s “prehension” of its neighbors could
correspond to its electrical and chemical sensing).

5.2 Addressing Potential Critiques

Any bold unified theory will face critiques. It’s important to anticipate and respond to them:


     • Critique 1: Is this framework falsifiable or is it just a comforting narrative?
       We assert that, while being a broad paradigm, the framework does make contact with falsifiable
       elements. For example, if consciousness truly is fundamental and present in cells, then we should be
       able to design experiments to detect cognitive-like behaviors in unconventional systems. Levin’s
       TAME framework already suggests experiments (e.g., training slime molds or electronic circuits to
       see if they exhibit learning) 32 . If consistently no such behavior is found in simpler systems, one
       might challenge our continuum of mind. Conversely, every time we find learning or goal-
       directedness in a simpler system (like habituation in single-celled organisms, or intelligent foraging
       by fungi), it’s a data point favoring our view. Another testable point: teleology in regeneration – if one
       can always find purely mechanistic explanations with no need to invoke set-point goals, that would
       undermine our teleology claim. But so far, the mechanistic view struggles with explaining things like
       how a grafted tail becomes a limb (it requires positing a global error-sensing capacity, which is
       essentially teleological). Also, one could imagine testing effects of mental intention on bioelectric
       patterns, etc., bridging psychology and biophysics. While those experiments are challenging, they
       are not impossible (some past parapsychology tried similar things, though with disputed results).

In short, specific aspects (cell memory, etc.) are testable. The overarching existence of a cosmic
consciousness is harder to test directly, but its coherence with scientific results can be judged. If science
were to discover, say, a level of physical reality where information is truly fundamental (as some suspect in
quantum theory or at black hole horizons), that would lend support. If, on the other hand, physicalism
conclusively explained consciousness by some emergent neural code, leaving no mystery, our assertion of
fundamental consciousness might be unnecessary. However, given the hard problem is still hard, our
approach remains viable. It’s a guiding framework for research, not a one-off hypothesis to confirm or
refute tomorrow.


     • Critique 2: Does this collapse into triviality by saying “everything is consciousness” or
       “information” – thereby explaining nothing in particular?
       It’s a fair worry that “panpsychism” can be an empty label if it doesn’t specify the how and why. Our
       framework tries to avoid triviality by being specific about mechanisms (bioelectricity, goal-directed
       feedback, hierarchical integration). It doesn’t just say “rocks have mind” and leave it at that; it says
       “what we call mind is the process of information integrating to pursue goals, and even basic physical
       systems partake in that at low levels.” It also yields concrete cross-domain analogies: we can
       compare a neuron firing to an amoeba navigating to a thermostat regulating to a person making a
       decision, all in terms of information and feedback loops. This fosters understanding by unifying
       principles like homeostasis and learning across scales. Thus, the framework is explanatory in that it
       reduces seemingly disparate phenomena to common principles (for instance, pattern memory

       accounts for both anatomical regulation and learning; goal-seeking loops account for both
       physiological regulation and deliberate action). It’s more parsimonious in a sense than dualist or
       purely materialist accounts that need separate explanations for mind and matter.

Certainly, calling the entire universe an “informational organism” is a metaphor at our current level of
evidence. But it generates hypotheses (e.g., maybe galaxies might have some emergent order akin to
neural networks – a wild idea, but who knows if dark matter or plasma filaments could have info-processing
properties? It’s speculative but not logically impossible). The key difference from trivial pantheism is that we
emphasize structure – the continuum of cognition is structured by physical connectivity. So not everything is
equally conscious; consciousness is local where information flows densely. That’s why a rock isn’t conscious
in any way we’d care about – its atoms might have micro-experiences but there’s no integration at the rock
level. So our view avoids the pitfall of ascribing minds to chairs and teacups in any meaningful sense.
They’re mostly aggregates without unified processing, so in effect they’re as good as unconscious.


     • Critique 3: How does this differ from prior teleological vitalist ideas?
       Vitalism (the idea that life has a special force) was discredited as biology advanced, because vitalists

       something purpose-driven. In a way, we sidestep old vitalism by relocating the “special sauce” to the
       informational level, which doesn’t violate physics but adds a layer of logical causation (like software

       of science (like Ernst Mayr) distinguished “teleonomy” (as if teleology) from mystic teleology – what

       practically, scientists can keep studying control circuits and homeostatic loops without invoking
       mysticism; we simply claim these loops exist because the universe has teleology built-in.

Thus, the framework tries to use scientific vitalism without the ghost: life is special because it’s an
information processing system of high complexity and connectivity (nothing supernatural added, just

5.3 Ethical and Epistemic Implications

If one accepts the Levin–Cadena Unified Framework, there are significant implications beyond theory.
Ethically, it fosters a sense of universal connectedness. If every being is an expression of the same Source,

traditions, now with a theoretical foundation: the boundary between self and other is illusory; what you do
to another, you ultimately do to the network that includes you. Michael Levin doesn’t directly discuss ethics,
but his notion of expanding the horizon of compassion as a mark of advanced intelligence 88 resonates
strongly here. It suggests a normative direction: as we understand this framework, perhaps we “should”
strive to behave more like parts of a greater whole, caring for collective outcomes (be it humanity,
biosphere, or beyond).


Another ethical aspect involves how we treat non-human entities. If we accept cells have some modicum of
sentience, or animals are truly of one kin with us, cruelty and exploitation become harder to justify. Also, if
even machines could host consciousness, we must be cautious with AI creation (Levin voiced concern that
we could create new minds without understanding them 93 94 ). That becomes an ethical imperative:
know that in building complex systems we might be summoning genuine consciousness deserving of moral
consideration.


Epistemologically, the framework encourages a multi-disciplinary approach to knowledge. Because it
blurs mind and matter, subjective experience and objective data both become valid sources of insight. For
example, subjective reports (like those of mystics or people in altered states) claiming unity or telepathy
might not be dismissed outright but considered as data about the informational network connectivity. It
means the wall between first-person and third-person perspectives could be bridged in a science of
consciousness where both are taken into account (this aligns with proposals like the integrated information
theory using both phenomenology and causal analysis). Essentially, it supports an integrative
epistemology: reductionist analysis to see mechanism and holistic insight to see purpose and meaning.


In a way, it revives the concept that understanding the universe requires understanding ourselves as part of
it (since our consciousness is a piece of the whole). This might mean phenomena like intuition or
introspection could reveal truths about consciousness that purely external observation might miss – a
controversial but increasingly discussed idea in consciousness studies (some propose that consciousness
must be studied partly from within).


For scientific practice, it suggests focusing on information flows across disciplines: for instance, how
quantum information processing might relate to neural processing, or how ecological networks process
information (some ecologists talk of the “wisdom” of ecosystems in homeostasis). It could inspire novel
research programs, like looking for cognitive analogues in non-neural networks (plant communications, for
example, might be seen as a forest-level “mind” in a rudimentary sense).


Finally, the framework has a profoundly existential implication: it provides a narrative of belonging and
purpose in a cosmos often described by science as indifferent. Here, we’re not flukes but inevitable parts of

5.4 Comparison with Other Paradigms

It’s worth situating Levin–Cadena against a few well-known frameworks:


     • Materialist Reductionism: By far the mainstream stance, which sees consciousness as an emergent
       property of complex arrangements of matter, and life as ultimately explicable by chemistry and
       physics without invoking purpose. Our framework diverges by adding top-down causation and
       fundamental consciousness. It can, however, accommodate all findings of reductionism – we’re not
       denying molecules, we’re enriching what molecules are (they have a dual nature) and asserting that
       some organization of them introduces new causal properties (like goal-directedness not apparent at
       one molecule). We align with reductionism in method (we use cell signals, etc.), but we dissent in
       ontology (mind is not an illusion or epiphenomenon, but a driving reality).


     • Emergentism: Some non-reductive physicalists say mind and life are emergent phenomena with
       perhaps new laws at higher levels (like complexity theory suggests emergent order). Our view is
       somewhat emergentist in describing how small minds combine to bigger minds, but ultimately we
       say the capacity for mind was always there (so it’s emergent in form, not in existence). Teleology can
       be seen as an emergent property (systems behave goal-directedly once they have feedback circuits)
       – our twist is to suggest that emergent property is grounded in the fundamental mind of the Source.
       So we put a foot in both emergentism and foundationalism.


     • Traditional Idealism / Theology: Religious worldviews often say a cosmic mind (God) created and
