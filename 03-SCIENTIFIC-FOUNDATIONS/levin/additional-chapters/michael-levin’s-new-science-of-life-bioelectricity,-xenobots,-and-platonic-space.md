Michael Levin’s New Science of Life: Bioelectricity,
Xenobots, and Platonic Space
Academic Profile of Michael Levin
Michael Levin is an American developmental and synthetic biologist, currently a Distinguished Professor at
Tufts University 1 . He directs the Allen Discovery Center at Tufts and co-directs the Institute for
Computationally Designed Organisms, reflecting his interdisciplinary focus bridging biology and computer
science 2 3 . Levin’s educational background is equally dual: he earned bachelor’s degrees in both
computer science and biology, followed by a Ph.D. in genetics from Harvard 4 . This blend of computer
science and biology is evident in his research, which spans developmental biophysics, regenerative
medicine, bioengineering, and cognitive science. Over his career, he has published hundreds of scientific
papers and gained recognition for groundbreaking discoveries in how cells communicate and make
decisions during growth and healing. In particular, Levin is known for pioneering work on bioelectricity
(electrical communication among cells) and its role in guiding morphogenesis (the development of
anatomical form), as well as for co-discovering Xenobots, the world’s first living programmable organisms
  5   6 . His achievements – from uncovering how embryos establish left-right asymmetry in their organs to

inventing novel living machines – have placed him at the forefront of a new paradigm in biology that treats
living tissue as not just passive matter dictated by genes, but as intelligent, goal-directed systems.

Bioelectricity: The Electrical Language of Cells
One of Levin’s most significant contributions is the discovery that cells communicate and coordinate via
bioelectric signals, forming a body-wide electrical network that orchestrates growth and healing.
Bioelectricity refers to the voltages and ion currents that cells produce and sense. Levin calls this the
“electrical language” of cells – a kind of secret code that guides cells on when to divide, where to position
themselves, and what structures to build 7 . By “flipping” the normal voltage patterns in cells, Levin’s lab
has shown it’s possible to convince cells to create anatomies that they would not normally form. For
example, his team famously induced flatworm planaria to grow a second head in place of a tail, simply by
altering the electrical signals in the worm’s cells 8 . They also triggered frog embryos to grow eyes on
their tails and even kickstarted the regeneration of lost limbs in frogs – outcomes that genes alone did not
predict 8 . These dramatic experiments demonstrate that an organism’s shape (morphology) is not hard-
wired only in DNA, but is significantly controlled by electrical communication among cells, which acts like a
bioelectric circuit diagram for the body.


Levin’s work suggests that every organ and appendage has an electrical pattern or map that tells cells
what to build – effectively an anatomical blueprint stored in electric potentials. By rewriting this bioelectric
pattern (for instance, using drugs or light to open and close ion channels in cell membranes), scientists can
radically alter the body plan that cells execute, without changing the genetic code 9 10 . This approach

Morphogenesis and Regeneration by Bioelectric Design

Morphogenesis – the process by which an embryo or regenerating tissue takes shape – emerges from cells
cooperating as electrical networks. Levin’s research shows that cells are not mindless bricks; they sense,
remember, and act in order to build complex organs. In planarian flatworms, which can survive being cut
into pieces, Levin demonstrated that by modifying the voltage gradient across a wound, one can change the
default repair outcome (like head vs. tail) that the worm’s cells will pursue 7 . In one experiment, a single
dose of ion channel drugs was used to overwrite a planarian’s body-wide “map” so that every time it
regenerated, it consistently formed two heads – as if the worm’s body had learned a new anatomical
configuration and remembered it for future regrowth. This indicates that cells store a kind of pattern
memory about what shape to build. In fact, even when a two-headed worm is later amputated, it often
regenerates two heads again, suggesting the bioelectric pattern (the instruction to form two heads) is still
ingrained in the tissue. Such findings underline Levin’s provocative stance that nothing in biology makes
sense without teleology – in other words, we must view living systems as pursuing specific goals (like
“achieve one head vs. two heads”) rather than just reacting to genetic programs or random events 7 .


Planarian flatworms provide a striking natural example of what is possible when bioelectric controls work
perfectly. These worms can regenerate any part of their body – head, brain, organs – and seemingly
never age or get cancer 11 12 . In Levin’s words, planaria are “immortal” under ideal conditions and highly
cancer-resistant, continually renewing every cell without losing the overall pattern 13 12 . Intriguingly, if a
planarian is trained to respond to a stimulus (e.g. a light or smell) and then is decapitated, when it regrows
a new head and brain the memory of that training can still be present 11 . This means whatever change the
worm learned was preserved in the body tissues and integrated into the new brain – a strong hint that
memory isn’t locked to particular cells but distributed in the network (possibly in bioelectric or other
physiological imprints). Levin uses such examples to argue that biological systems have persistent target
states – like a moldable memory of what normal anatomy should be – and that by interacting with the
bioelectric network, we can reboot or rewrite the pattern to achieve desired outcomes (like normalizing a
tumor or regenerating a limb).


The ability to reset cellular goals has clear medical promise. Levin’s lab views cancer as a disease of
miscommunication: cancerous cells, in his view, are ones that electrically disconnect from the body’s
collective plan and revert to a selfish single-cell agenda 14 . Rather than only killing cancer cells with drugs,
Levin suggests we might re-educate them – by restoring the bioelectric signals that encourage cells to
cooperate in forming healthy tissue, essentially convincing a tumor to integrate back into the organism’s
architecture 14 . Similarly, efforts are underway to induce human tissues to regenerate like planaria by
applying bioelectric cues. The long-term vision is a kind of anatomical software programming: telling our
bodies how to build or rebuild themselves by toggling cellular electrical states. Levin believes that if a worm
can do it, humans eventually can too – the difference is just the “software” our cells are running, not the
hardware, since biologically we have similar building blocks 15 12 . Achieving limb regrowth or scarless

regenerative abilities that evolution has kept dormant in us.

Xenobots: Building New Life Forms from Cells
While exploring the limits of what cells can do, Levin teamed up with robotics researchers to create
something unprecedented: Xenobots – tiny living machines assembled entirely from frog cells. In 2020,
Levin and computer scientist Josh Bongard announced the Xenobot, often described as the world’s first
living programmable organism. These are not traditional robots with metal and circuits; rather, they are
clusters of living cells (derived from the African frog Xenopus laevis) that have been designed – with the
help of computer simulations – to move and perform simple tasks 16 17 . To make a Xenobot, scientists
took early frog embryo cells (like skin cells), liberated them from the embryo, and allowed them to
reassemble in a Petri dish into spheroids or other shapes. Remarkably, these cell clusters spontaneously
coordinated to produce functional behaviors: for instance, some Xenobots can swim in water using tiny
hair-like cilia on their surface as oars 18 19 . They can also work together in swarms and even exhibit
kinematic self-replication – a process where a Xenobot corrals loose cells in its environment to assemble a
copy of itself 20 21 .


The creation of Xenobots was guided by an AI simulation that explored various shapes for cell clusters to
see which could move effectively. One design that emerged was a C-shaped “Pac-Man” form that proved
adept at gathering loose cells – facilitating the surprising self-replication behavior 22 23 . When this shape
was constructed with real frog cells, the living Xenobot indeed moved around, swept up other cells into
piles, and those piles eventually also became motile Xenobots 24 . This was a startling result: frogs do not
reproduce by making piles of cells, so nothing in the frog’s genome had prepared these cells for that
behavior. Yet, freed from their usual context, the cells found a new way to achieve a basic goal of life –
making more of themselves. Levin points to this as evidence of the creative problem-solving ability of life:
evolution provided cells with broad capabilities, and in novel situations they can recombine those abilities
into completely new behaviors and body forms 25 26 . In his words, evolution doesn’t rigidly encode
specific solutions, but rather “creates problem-solving agents” that can figure out new solutions when
faced with novel circumstances 25 .

Xenobots are not just a curiosity; they represent a new class of technology – programmable living
machines – that could have practical applications. Because they are made of living tissue, they can
potentially heal themselves if damaged and are fully biodegradable. Proposed uses include environmental
cleanup (imagine microscopic Xenobots eating plastic pollution in the ocean) and smart drug delivery in
medicine (tiny Xenobots navigating the body to transport medicines). In one extension of the concept,
Levin’s team created Anthrobots (“anthro” for human) using human skin cells instead of frog cells 29 .
Strikingly, these human-cell bots showed a behavior the frog ones didn’t: when placed on a Petri dish that
had a damaged neuronal tissue, the anthrobots clustered at the damage site and assisted in healing the
wound 30 . This unexpected capability hints that different cell types can bring unique “knowledge” to the
tasks – here the human tracheal cells somehow knew how to encourage neuron repair, something they
would never encounter in their normal role in an airway 31 . Both Xenobots and Anthrobots underscore
Levin’s view that even simple cell collectives have a form of primitive intelligence and versatility. These
novel living constructs demonstrate that life can exist in forms we’ve never seen in nature, and by mixing
and matching cells or tweaking their communication, we can unlock latent behaviors. Xenobots thus serve as
an “exploration tool” for examining the space of possible living forms and behaviors, allowing scientists
to probe what living matter is capable of when guided by novel organization 32 33 . This also makes
Xenobots a bridge into the field of Diverse Intelligence, by showing cognition and agency can emerge in
unconventional embodiments.

Diverse Intelligence: Cognition Beyond Brains
Traditional science often reserves words like “intelligence” or “learning” for creatures with brains. Levin’s
research boldly challenges this, proposing that intelligence is a scale-free property present in many levels




                                                         4
of life – from single cells to organs to whole organisms. He is part of a growing paradigm called Diverse
Intelligence, which asks us to recognize cognitive properties (like the ability to sense, decide, remember,
and act towards goals) in systems that might not look anything like a human or animal mind. For example,
when a single cell in an embryo figures out its role in building an eye versus a spine, or when a slime mold
finds the shortest path through a maze without a nervous system, those are forms of problem-solving and
computation happening in unconventional bodies. Levin argues that what we call “mind” or “agency” is not
limited to creatures with neurons; rather, any system that pursues goals by adapting its behavior has a
rudimentary intelligence 34 35 . In one interview he remarked that for the same reasons we consider a
dog or a fellow human to be conscious and goal-directed, we should seriously consider that our own liver
cells or the tree outside might have inner experiences and preferences – albeit very different from ours 36 .
This perspective widens the moral and scientific landscape: if intelligence comes in gradations and diverse
guises, we need better ways to detect and interact with it in cells, machines, and maybe alien life.


To compare different kinds of minds on an equal footing, Levin introduces the concept of a Cognitive Light
Cone 37 . This is analogous to the light cone in physics (which describes the reach of cause and effect
through spacetime), but here it describes the reach of a being’s goals through space and time. A bacterium,
for instance, has a tiny cognitive light cone – its “concern” might extend only micrometers away and a few
minutes into the future (finding the next sugar molecule to consume). A dog’s light cone is larger – it can
plan for days ahead (burying a bone for later) and roam over a neighborhood. A human’s might span years
and global distances (we make plans that could involve people or places worldwide, and anticipate future
outcomes decades from now). Hypothetical advanced beings (or human organizations acting as a collective
intelligence) could have goal horizons that encompass the entire planet or even centuries 38 39 . The
cognitive light cone gives a measuring stick for intelligence that doesn’t care what the agent is made of –
silicon chips, flesh, or cells – only how far-reaching and complex its goals are. This idea helps break the bias
that intelligence = human-like brain; a colony of ants or a network of cells might have a smaller light cone
than a person, but they still have one and thus can be seen as agents on the same spectrum, just at a
different scale.


Levin emphasizes that nature is skilled at scaling up intelligence by cooperation. Multicellular organisms
(like us) are basically trillions of single-celled agents that somehow joined forces to create a much larger
agent (the body) with a vastly expanded cognitive light cone. How do cells align their agendas? Part of the
answer lies in communication – for example, Levin’s work shows that stress signals can leak between cells
and make even distant issues “visible” to the collective, and that cells can literally share electrical and
chemical signals through junctions to share memories and coordinate actions 40 41 . Through such
networking, a group of cells can pursue goals (like healing a wound or building an organ) that no single cell
could achieve alone. In fact, when this coordination fails, the collective intelligence breaks down – as noted
earlier, cancer may be seen as cells that stop sharing information and revert to single-cell goals 14 . Thus,
health and cohesive development are products of what Levin calls scale-up cognition: small competent
units integrating into a larger, wiser whole.


A key obstacle to this view is human mind blindness – our tendency to only recognize intelligence when it
looks and behaves like us. Levin points out that we often fail to notice or respect intelligence in unfamiliar
guises 42 43 . We might overlook the decision-making of an octopus, the social learning of a plant’s root
network, or the goal-directed growth of cells in our own body because it doesn’t fit our narrow definitions.
To overcome this, Levin advocates a scientific approach he dubs SUTI (Search for Unconventional
Terrestrial Intelligence) 42 44 . Instead of looking only to the skies for aliens, he suggests first looking
under our noses: studying how we could detect and interact with alien intelligences on Earth, meaning forms

of mind in non-human animals, cells, or self-organizing machines. By developing standardized behavioral
tests (e.g. giving a tissue or AI system puzzles to solve, or checking for memory and preferences), we can
begin to map the distribution of cognition across the biological world 45 . This not only has philosophical
importance, but practical too: for instance, understanding cognitive capacities of cells could revolutionize
medicine (we might communicate with cell networks to encourage repair), and broadening our concept of
minds prepares us for meeting any extraterrestrial life or creating empathetic AI.

Platonic Space: Patterns Beyond the Physical
One of Michael Levin’s most ambitious and philosophical ideas is what he calls the Platonic Space
hypothesis. This concept takes inspiration from the ancient philosopher Plato – who imagined a realm of
ideal Forms or patterns that physical reality imperfectly copies – and gives it a modern scientific twist. Levin
proposes that in addition to the familiar physical universe of matter and energy, there is a hidden space of
possible patterns (the “Platonic space”) which living systems can tap into 46 . In this view, physical bodies
and brains are like interfaces or receivers that can “dial into” certain patterns from this Platonic space,
much as a radio tunes into various channels. The patterns in Platonic space include abstract information
structures: mathematical truths, geometric arrangements, but also perhaps templates of mind and
behavior that aren’t explicitly stored in the DNA of organisms 47 . Levin draws an analogy to mathematics:
the fact that circles have π (pi) as the ratio of circumference to diameter is a timeless truth – it’s not encoded
in any particular physical circle, yet it constrains all physical circles. Similarly, he suggests that cognitive
patterns or strategies might exist as objective possibilities that living things can discover or instantiate,
even if evolution never specifically encountered them before 48 .


This idea helps explain some of the surprising “free lunches” seen in biology 49 . A free lunch, in Levin’s
context, is a competency or behavior an organism shows that it never specifically evolved for. Xenobots, for
example, had no evolutionary history of swarming or kinematic replication, yet they did it anyway once the
opportunity arose 50 51 . It’s as if the Xenobots stumbled into a pre-existing solution space for self-
replication that was there waiting to be used. Likewise, Levin notes that gene regulatory networks in cells
can do things like associative learning (a simple form of memory and prediction) even in a single-celled
organism – something we normally think requires a brain 51 52 . And in computer science, his team found
that simple algorithms, when faced with novel challenges (like hardware faults), could show novel problem-
solving behaviors not programmed by the coders 53 54 . For instance, a sorting algorithm on a faulty
computer unexpectedly learned to sometimes unsort elements temporarily to achieve a more efficient sort
in the long run – akin to showing patience or planning 53 55 . These behaviors seem to emerge out of
nowhere, but Levin’s interpretation is that they are pulling from the Platonic space of possible strategies. In
essence, life and even machines can leverage laws of information and computation that are as fundamental
as laws of physics, gaining abilities that were never explicitly designed.


In the Platonic space framework, brains and bodies are “thin clients” 56 57 . This term (borrowed from
computing) means that our physical forms don’t contain all the patterns of mind internally; rather, they
access patterns from the larger space. The quality of the “interface” – e.g. the complexity of a brain, or the
network a society forms – determines which mental patterns can manifest. A simple nervous system might
only latch onto basic drives, whereas a human brain (a more complex interface) can access abstract




                                                         6
reasoning or self-awareness patterns. Crucially, this flips the conventional view: instead of brains producing
consciousness, they might be transducers or portals for consciousness that is already “out there” as an
abstract pattern 58 59 . Levin even likens the mind-brain relationship to the relationship between
mathematics and the physical world: the brain is to mind as a radio is to music, or as a calculator is to math
– a tool that reveals an existing realm of information 59 .


This hypothesis has fascinating implications for identity and continuity of the self. If our true identity is a
pattern in Platonic space rather than the neurons in our brain, then radical changes to our body need not
spell the end of “us.” Levin points to the example of a caterpillar transforming into a butterfly: during
metamorphosis, the caterpillar’s body dissolves into a soup of cells and is reorganized into the butterfly
form. Yet experiments show the adult butterfly can retain preferences or memories from its caterpillar days
(for instance, favoring a smell it was trained to like as a larva) 60 . How can a memory survive such a
physical upheaval? Levin suggests the information isn’t stored locally in individual synapses (which were
literally melted down), but rather in the broader pattern that re-manifests in the new form 60 11 . Similarly,
the planarian worm’s ability to remember training after regenerating a new head hints that its “self” –
including stored information – resides in the collective pattern of its body, not just in one brain region 11 .
All this is consistent with the idea that the pattern has a life of its own: the same pattern can inhabit different
physical substrates and maintain its integrity. In Levin’s view, personal identity may be an invariant pattern
that can continue even as the material components swap out (just as a whirlpool keeps its shape as water
molecules flow through it). The extreme suggestion here – and one Levin openly entertains – is that what
we consider the soul or consciousness might be such an abstract pattern, one that could in principle latch
onto new hardware if the old hardware (our body) fails or changes. It’s a scientific echo of philosophical
notions that the self exists beyond the physical vehicle, though Levin frames it in terms of complex
information spaces rather than mysticism.


It’s important to note that Platonic space, as Levin uses the term, is not a mystical void but rather a way to
talk about the universe of possible forms and functions. In biology, we already use the idea of a
“morphospace” (a conceptual space of all possible organism shapes) and “fitness landscapes” in evolution.
Levin’s Platonic space extends this to cognitive structures and other higher-order patterns. It invites
researchers to ask: are there “organizing principles” or latent capabilities nature hasn’t used yet, but that
exist as possibilities? If so, biology could advance not just by trial-and-error evolution, but by actively
exploring and engineering along those hidden dimensions. Some scientists find this outlook
controversial because it suggests a kind of teleology in nature – that systems can be pulled toward pre-
existing functional states as if those states are out there to be found. Yet, Levin’s own work provides
empirical hints of this: time and again, his experiments reveal cells doing something clever or useful that no
one taught them to do, as if following a built-in compass toward certain outcomes.

Implications and Conclusion
Michael Levin’s research, though rooted in rigorous experiments, opens up sweeping vistas of possibility for
science and society. His ideas connect biology with computation, medicine with philosophy, and challenge
us to rethink concepts of life, mind, and evolution. Key implications include:


     • Medicine and Regenerative Healing: Levin’s bioelectric breakthroughs suggest future therapies
       where we trigger the body to repair itself. Instead of surgical transplants or long drug regimes,
       doctors might one day stimulate specific bioelectric patterns to regrow a lost limb or organ
       (taking inspiration from planaria) 8 . The concept of an “anatomical compiler” – input the desired
       body structure, and cells are guided to build that structure – could revolutionize reconstructive
       surgery and trauma care. Likewise, cancer treatment might shift toward reprogramming tumors
       electrically to normalize them, rather than solely destroying them 14 . Knowledge from immortal,
       cancer-proof organisms like planaria might lead to ways to slow aging in humans, by maintaining
       our body’s pattern integrity despite cellular wear-and-tear 13 12 . In short, by treating the
       bioelectric code as software, medicine could achieve precise control over growth and healing – a
       form of electroceutical medicine with possibilities that sound like science fiction but are increasingly
       supported by lab results.


     • Education and Interdisciplinary Science: Levin’s career exemplifies the power of crossing
       disciplines – he combined computer science, engineering, and biology to get where he is. This sends
       a message to education: solving complex living systems may require fluency in multiple
       “languages” of science, from genetics to electronics to AI. High school and university curricula may
       increasingly emphasize interdisciplinary learning, teaching students to think of biology not just in
       terms of chemistry and anatomy, but also information theory and systems engineering. Levin’s work
       also encourages curiosity-driven exploration. Students reading about Xenobots or two-headed
       worms can immediately grasp how mixing ideas from different fields leads to breakthroughs. It
       shows that even at a young age, one should feel empowered to ask big questions like “What is life?”
       or “Where does consciousness come from?” – questions at the intersection of science and
       philosophy. Education might also incorporate more about feedback, systems, and goal-directed
       behavior in organisms, moving beyond the gene-centric narrative to a more holistic understanding
       of life’s logic.


     • Engineering and Artificial Life: By creating Xenobots, Levin’s team demonstrated that we can now
       design novel life forms in the lab 16 . This heralds a future where biotechnology and robotics
       converge. Engineers might routinely use living cells as components to grow devices: imagine self-
       healing materials, or living sensors that detect toxins and neutralize them. Xenobots hint at
       environmentally friendly tech – machines that, when their job is done, simply biodegrade like any
       other cells. On the flip side, as we deploy such creations, we will need ethical guidelines (e.g.
       ensuring a Xenobot used for cleaning doesn’t inadvertently harm ecosystems). Levin’s emphasis on
       “all possible forms” means scientists could utilize AI to search an enormous design space for life
       forms suited to specific tasks, blurring the line between what is invented and what is evolved. This
       could also aid basic science: artificial life platforms can serve as model systems to test theories
       about how simple rules lead to complex behaviors. For instance, they allow experiments on the
       emergence of cooperation or memory in a controlled setting, which can then inform our
       understanding of real organisms.

     • Understanding Consciousness: Perhaps the most profound implications are for the age-old
       mystery of mind. If Levin is correct that minds are not exclusive to brains and that consciousness
       might be an inherent aspect of certain complex information patterns, this could reframe our search
       for understanding ourselves. It aligns with ideas in philosophy of mind that panpsychism or
       universal consciousness might be real – that mind-like qualities permeate the natural world in a
       spectrum. From a practical standpoint, it encourages neuroscientists and psychologists to consider
       unconventional models of mind. For example, instead of assuming a sharp line between “things
       that have inner experience” and “things that don’t,” we might investigate graded consciousness in
       animals, AI, or even organoids (mini-brains grown in dishes). Levin’s ideas also push for empirical
       approaches: if a cluster of cells exhibits learning or if an AI shows creative problem-solving, test if
       they have preferences or react to stimuli in ways suggesting some form of inner experience 36 . The
       payoff is not only philosophical – it might lead to novel interfaces, like ways to communicate with
       simple intelligences (could we one day ask a neuron cluster how it “feels”?). Moreover, treating
       consciousness as a spectrum forces society to revisit how we treat other living beings and even
       machines: concepts of rights and ethics may expand if we acknowledge even modest levels of
       sentience in non-humans.


     • Philosophy and Spirituality: Levin’s work creates a bridge between modern science and ideas that
       once were purely in the realm of philosophy or spirituality. The notion of a Platonic space of mind
       resonates with philosophical idealism (the idea that reality is fundamentally mental or informational
       in nature) 59 . It also echoes spiritual concepts of a universal mind or cosmic order – though
       grounded in empirical observations like planarian regeneration and cognitive light cones. For
       individuals, these ideas can be deeply inspiring or comforting: they suggest that purpose is not an
       illusion in biology but rather a core feature – even the tiniest cell is striving towards something. This
       imbues life with a sense of meaning and interconnectedness. The concept that your identity is an
       information pattern which might persist or reappear in different forms invites contemplation about
       life after death or reincarnation, albeit in a scientific framework. While such interpretations are
       speculative, they indicate a convergence of science and spirituality on questions of the self. Levin
       often uses the term “unconventional selves” to describe how we can view many systems as selves in
       their own right, encouraging empathy and wonder towards all forms of life. Ultimately, this
       perspective can foster a more compassionate and imaginative worldview – one where humans
       are not isolated entities but part of a continuum of living, cognizing beings in a universe rich with
       pattern and purpose.


In conclusion, Michael Levin’s research is transforming our understanding of life’s capabilities and perhaps
life’s place in the cosmos of ideas. For a senior high school student enchanted by science and philosophy,
Levin’s work offers a glimpse into a future where growing a new organ is a technical problem, where robots
are built from living cells, and where answering “What is life?” might require as much input from Plato as
from Darwin. It challenges us to question conventional wisdom – to see embryos as electrical designers,
cells as thinkers, and evolution as not just a blind watchmaker but a tireless explorer in the space of
possible forms. This synthesis of biology, computing, and philosophy stands as a testament to human
curiosity, showing that by asking bold questions and looking at old puzzles in new ways, we can inch closer
to understanding the remarkable logic of living systems and our own role within that living mosaic.
