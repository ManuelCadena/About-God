Summary 
This interview mainly discussed Professor Michael Levin's research on bioelectricity, cognition, and the nature of intelligence across biological systems. Curt Jaimungal hosted the conversation, which explored Levin's perspective on different levels of explanation in biology beyond the molecular level. Levin explained his concept of "cognitive light cones" - the scope of goals an agent can pursue - and how this relates to cancer, regeneration, and collective intelligence. He discussed how cells communicate electrically to form larger cognitive systems and how this understanding leads to practical biomedical applications. Levin shared the history of his lab's discoveries since 2000, including manipulating bioelectric states to control organ development, induce regeneration, and normalize cancer cells. He described creating xenobots and anthrobots (living robots made from frog and human cells), which demonstrate novel behaviors without genetic modification. Throughout the interview, Levin emphasized that intelligence exists across various biological systems beyond brains, that material substrates are more capable of cognition than traditionally thought, and that understanding these principles leads to practical medical applications like regeneration therapies and cancer treatments.

Which one do you feel like is on the right track? (regarding theories of consciousness) ‎‎ 01:03:44
Levin acknowledged he doesn't have a new theory of consciousness to offer. He explained that the hard problem of consciousness is difficult because we don't know what the output of a correct theory would look like - if it outputs patterns of behavior or physiological states, critics would say it hasn't explained consciousness itself. He suggested that for the same reasons we associate consciousness with brains, we should take seriously the possibility of other forms of consciousness in other parts of our bodies and in other systems. 
What is it you agree with him the most that you think most people would disagree about? (regarding Bernardo Kastrup) ‎‎ 01:10:11
Levin mentioned they agree on the primacy of consciousness and that Kastrup's idealist position has merit. He said they disagree on compositionality - Kastrup believes that to be a true self with conscious experiences requires being individualized from the start, while Levin sees this as a contingent feature of biology. Levin believes boundaries between self and world are more fluid and can change throughout development. 
Does the world have agency? ‎‎ 01:14:22
Levin framed this as an empirical question rather than a philosophical one. He explained that determining agency requires hypothesizing what problem space something is working in, what its goals might be, and then conducting experiments to test these hypotheses. He suggested that while some systems (like planetary-scale ones) are difficult to experiment on, in principle we could design experiments to test for cognitive properties at various scales. 
Take me through the history of the 11 lab. ‎‎ 01:18:57
Levin recounted that his lab began in September 2000 at Harvard Medical School. He described key milestones including: early work showing bioelectricity's role in left-right asymmetry (2002); discovering that bioelectric states can instruct cells to build eyes; finding that serotonin helps cells interpret electrical signals before the nervous system develops; discovering the "electric face" pattern in embryonic development (2007-2008); showing that bioelectricity drives regeneration in tadpole tails; demonstrating that cancer has bioelectric components (2010-2011); creating two-headed planarian worms through bioelectric manipulation; developing xenobots from frog cells and later anthrobots from human cells; and advancing regeneration research toward mammalian applications. 
What is meant when you say 11 lab? ‎‎ 01:50:00
Levin explained that when scientists become faculty members, they get their own lab space and research group. The "11 lab" refers to all the research controlled by him as the principal investigator. He mentioned having three different physical locations on campus where his research is conducted, all considered part of the 11 lab because he's responsible for bringing in funding, hiring people, and determining what research happens. 
What advice do you have for a newcomer to biology? ‎‎ 02:04:12
Levin jokingly said step one is to ignore most people's advice. He distinguished between specific critiques of work (which are valuable for improvement) versus large-scale career advice (which he thinks should generally be ignored). He emphasized that people should develop their own intuition about what to pursue rather than following others' opinions about what they should be working on. He noted that many of his lab's most interesting discoveries came from pursuing ideas that others had advised against.
