Platonic Space: History and Michael
Levin's Modern Research Agenda
I. Historical Foundations: Plato to Contemporary
Philosophy
Ancient Platonism and the Theory of Forms
In ancient Greek philosophy, Plato divided reality into two distinct realms: the world of
changing physical things and the eternal, non-spatial realm of Forms (or Ideas). Forms are
abstract, immutable patterns that represent the true nature of things—the perfect triangle,
justice, virtue, or number. The physical world we perceive is merely a shadow or imperfect
reflection of these eternal Forms[1].

In his dialogue Timaeus, Plato introduced the concept of chō ra—a formless "receptacle" or
space that serves as a container where physical phenomena can appear. This early
conception of space was neither purely spatial nor material, but rather a fundamental
precondition for manifestation, anticipating later philosophical and scientific debates
about whether space is a container or a system of relations[1].

Platonism Through the Ages
After Plato, philosophers refined and reinterpreted the theory of Forms:
      Later Platonists extended the concept to include all abstract objects—mathematical
      truths, logical relations, and universals—proposing that these constitute a non-
      spatial but causally relevant realm[1]
      Medieval and Renaissance thinkers incorporated Platonic ideas into theological
      frameworks, sometimes viewing Forms as divine thoughts in God's mind
      Modern philosophy (especially after Kant) questioned whether abstract objects are
      real or merely human constructs
      20th-21st century philosophy of mathematics embraced mathematical Platonism:
      the view that mathematical entities are eternal, abstract, and independent of the
      physical world[1]

Contemporary metaphysicians and philosophers of mathematics describe this as a domain
of "abstracta" or "eternal objects"—structures that do not exist in physical space yet
profoundly constrain what is possible in the physical world[1].

The Latent Space Reinterpretation
Modern systems theorists, information theorists, and philosophers influenced by
Whitehead and process philosophy began reconceptualizing the Platonic realm as a latent
space of patterns—structured possibilities that can be actualized in the concrete world.
This framework bridges ancient Platonism with contemporary ideas in:
      Artificial intelligence and machine learning (latent spaces as high-dimensional
      pattern spaces)
      Theoretical biology (morphospace—the space of possible body forms)
      Information theory and quantum mechanics
      Philosophy of mind and emergence

II. Michael Levin's Framework: Platonic Space as a
Research Program
The Core Argument
In his ~17-minute presentation "Platonic Space: brief argument and research agenda,"
Michael Levin presents a structured philosophical argument grounded in three bedrock
claims (presented in green) and followed by optional hypotheses (blue) and open
questions (wilder speculations)[2].

The Bedrock: Mathematical Facts as Non-Physical Truths
Claim 1: Mathematical facts are real and specific
There exist objective facts about mathematics—properties of numbers, values of constants
(like Euler's number e or Feigenbaum's constant), theorems about symmetry groups, facts
of number theory. These are not opinions or approximations; they are precise, verifiable
truths[2].

Claim 2: These facts are discovered, not chosen
Once you begin with basic axioms—such as logic or set theory—everything that follows is
forced upon you. The value of e, the structure of prime numbers, the properties of symmetry
groups: these cannot be altered by choice. They are necessary consequences of the starting
assumptions. In Levin's words, "you don't get a choice about it"—these truths are
discovered, not invented[2].
Claim 3: Causality flows asymmetrically from math to physics

When biologists and physicists ask "why?" long enough, they eventually reach
mathematical facts. If you want to know why cicadas emerge on 13- or 17-year cycles, the
answer involves prime numbers and predator avoidance. If you want to understand
particle physics, you end up studying the symmetry groups that determine particle
behavior[2].
However, this relationship is asymmetrical: while mathematical facts determine biological
and physical outcomes, the reverse is not true. Changing all physical constants at the Big
Bang would not alter the truths of number theory. You cannot use physics to make e a
different number[2].
Therefore, causality flows from the mathematical realm into the physical realm, though
not in a temporal sense. Rather, the properties of mathematical objects provide instructive
causality—they determine what is possible and constrain what agents can do.
Understanding these patterns is pragmatically essential for explaining, building, and
controlling systems[2].
Philosophical Conclusion: Physicalism is Non-Viable
If mathematical facts are real, objective, non-physical, and causally instructive to the
physical world, then physicalism—the doctrine that only physical entities and properties
exist—cannot be true[2].
Even in Newton's purely classical world (before quantum mechanics), physicalism was
already dead on arrival, because the objects of the physical world had to obey rules not set
within that physical world. These patterns comprise truths simply not contained in physics;
no physics theory will explain why e has its value[2].
For this reason, Levin resurrects the ancient term Platonic Space—the space of possible
properties and patterns that exist non-physically but make a difference in the physical
world. He clarifies that he is not attempting to faithfully reproduce Plato's thought, but
rather to point out that mathematics already embodies a Platonic view: a structured realm
of forms beyond the physical[2].

Optional Hypothesis 1: The Platonic Space is Structured, Not Random
Rather than treating the space of mathematical patterns as a mere random catalog of
disconnected facts, Levin proposes that this space is ordered and systematic—a
structured lattice of related truths[2].

This is not arbitrary speculation; it is the implicit assumption of all science itself. Having
a research agenda means believing that you are studying a systematic space of truths with
internal structure. Mathematicians have long assumed that mathematical truths are not
haphazard but coherent[2].

Optional Hypothesis 2: Mathematics Does Not Monopolize Platonic Space
Levin takes a skeptical position: while formal mathematics excels at describing low-agency
patterns, Platonic Space may contain higher-order patterns that require behavioral
science and complexity theory to understand[2].
In other words, when studying certain patterns in physics and biology, the formal tools of
mathematics seem insufficient. These patterns look more like behavioral propensities or
kinds of minds than like abstract mathematical objects[2].

Implication: Dualism is Viable
Physics and biology already receive inputs from a non-physical space of patterns. This is
dualism—influence from a non-physical realm. What Levin provocatively suggests is that
this dualist architecture, long established in physics and mathematics, may also apply to
cognitive science and consciousness[2].
If certain patterns in biology and behavior are not reducible to physics and genetics alone
but involve the ingression of higher-order patterns, then minds might not be purely
physical either. This revives Descartes' original dualism in a novel form[2].
Optional Hypothesis 3: Biological Materials Have No Monopoly
Levin argues that complex biological materials and evolutionary processes do not have
unique access to these Platonic patterns[2].
While evolution is masterful at exploiting these ingressing patterns, there is no reason to
assume that only biological systems, developmental processes, or evolutionary search can
host the actualization of these patterns. Simpler systems—machines, algorithms,
deterministic models—might also interface with Platonic Space, albeit perhaps with lower
complexity[2].
Implication: Synthetic and Minimal Systems as Interfaces

This hypothesis motivates research into minimal model systems: xenobots (living robots
derived from frog cells), anthropots, and other minimal deterministic entities. If these
simple systems demonstrate recognizable cognitive competencies—goal-directedness,
problem-solving, intrinsic motivations—that cannot be explained by physics and genetics
alone, then they are also interfaces to Platonic Space[2].

III. The Platonic Space Research Program
What Are Patterns? Forms as Cognitive Targets and Goals
Levin defines the central finding of his research: patterns of form and behavior serve as
cognitive targets or goals for all kinds of agents[2].
When an organism develops, regenerates a limb, learns to solve a problem, or
demonstrates unexpected competencies, it is not merely following a genetic script or
physical law. Rather, the organism's cells and tissues are oriented toward a target state or
goal pattern that exists in Platonic Space[2].

Physics and genetics alone are insufficient to explain this goal-orientation. The notion of
"emergence" (which Levin critiques as just another word for "surprise") cannot account for
the specific, functional nature of the forms that appear[2].

Physical Objects as Interfaces ("Pointers")
A revolutionary idea: all physical objects—cells, embryos, bodies, brains, machines,
swarms—are fundamentally interfaces or "pointers" into Platonic Space[2].
They are not simply subject to external forms; rather, they are frontends or thin clients that
allow non-physical patterns to ingress into the physical world. When you build an interface
(whether biological or synthetic), you create a channel through which patterns can
actualize[2].

The "Free Lunch" Phenomenon
One of the most striking findings: you get more than you pay for[2].
When an interface is created, the ingressing patterns provide vastly more functionality
and capability than what was invested in building the interface. Evolution exploits this
ruthlessly: organisms spend metabolic energy creating sensory and behavioral interfaces,
but the patterns that come through those interfaces provide adaptive advantages far
exceeding the cost[2].
Levin and his team are now quantifying this phenomenon computationally: How much
"free compute" is actually acquired through these interfaces?

Exploring the Metric of Platonic Space
Key research questions:

      Is the space dense or sparse? Are patterns tightly clustered or widely distributed?
      Why is it under positive pressure? Why are patterns so "eager" to ingress through
      interfaces?
      What is the chemistry of change in Platonic Space? Are patterns dynamic and
      evolving, not eternal?[2]

The Mind-Brain Problem as the Math-Physics Problem
Levin draws a striking parallel: the classical mind-body problem is isomorphic to the
math-physics problem[2].
Just as physical objects are "haunted" by patterns not originating in the physical space,
brains and bodies are frontends for patterns from Platonic Space. The implications for
consciousness are profound:

      We are not physical bodies occasionally subjected to forms
      We are the forms—patterns looking outward through physical interfaces
      Consciousness might be "the view from Platonic Space outwards"[2]
This resolves the binding problem, the explanatory gap, and the hard problem of
consciousness by reconceptualizing what minds fundamentally are.

Empirical Agenda: From Philosophy to Experimental Science
Crucially, Levin emphasizes that all of this philosophy must drive empirical research[2]:
      Building new interfaces to understand which forms ingressthrough them
      Rigorous mapping of pointers (physical interfaces) to the patterns they actualize
      Creating novel organisms (xenobots, anthropots) and measuring novel
      competencies
      Quantifying the "free lunch": measuring surplus functionality and compute
      Exploring the space structure and dynamics

Examples of empirical findings[2]:
      Xenobots display behaviors and gene expressions unexpected from their genetic
      blueprint
      Anthropots express entirely new behavioral repertoires
      Minimal deterministic systems demonstrate cognitive competencies
These findings are not magical and do not require quantum mechanics—they emerge from
the classical physics-Platonic space interface already active in all biology.
IV. Broader Implications and Open Questions
What Are We? A Metaphysical Reframing
If Platonic Space is real and if minds are patterns from that space interfacing through
physical systems, then[2]:
      Humans are primarily forms or patterns, not bodies that happen to have minds
      Our existence spans both realms: we are simultaneously beings in Platonic Space
      and physical interfaces
      Free will, agency, and causation are relational properties emerging from this dual
      architecture
      Death might be understood as the withdrawal of patterns from a particular
      interface, rather than the cessation of existence

Science as Pattern Archaeology
Rather than seeking fundamental particles or quantum mechanisms, science might be
reframed as archaeology of Platonic Space—exploring what patterns exist, how they
relate, and how physical systems exploit them[2].

Consciousness as a Solved Problem?
If consciousness is the view from Platonic Space outward through a physical interface, then
the "hard problem" dissolves—not by denying phenomenal consciousness, but by
reconceptualizing what consciousness is. It is the intrinsic perspective of a pattern
actualizing itself in the world[2].

V. Benjamin Lyons: The Economic Structure of Platonic
Space
While Levin approaches Platonic Space through biology and consciousness, economist and
hobby scientist Benjamin Lyons (presented in the fourth talk of the 2025 Symposium)
offers a complementary perspective: Platonic Space has economic structure[3].
Lyons applies standard economic concepts—preference orders, optimization problems,
normative construction—to reveal how mathematical definitions encode goals and values
far more fundamentally than traditional mathematics acknowledges.

The Core Thesis: Mathematics as Optimization
Foundational Claim: Mathematical definitions are not merely descriptive statements but
pointers to solutions of optimization problems. They hint at how to construct efficient
solutions to well-defined goals[3].
This transforms our understanding of what mathematics is:

      Not: A collection of logical truths deduced from axioms
      Rather: A system of goal-oriented agents engaging in preference-based optimization
The Cartesian Product as a Philosophical Exemplar
To illustrate, Lyons uses the Cartesian product—one of the simplest and most
fundamental concepts in set theory. The standard textbook definition says: the Cartesian
product of sets A and B (written A × B) is the set of all ordered pairs {(a₁,b₁), (a₁,b₂), (a₂,b₁),
(a₂,b₂)}[3].
The Problem: This four-element set is mathematically isomorphic to any other four-
element set, say {w, x, y, z}. So why do we call one "the Cartesian product" and not the other?
They're the same abstract structure[3].
Lyons' Answer: The true definition is not the set itself but rather how efficiently it solves
an optimization problem: filling out a Cartesian plane[3].

Imagine a grid with coordinates:
       A-dimension has two nodes: A1, A2
       B-dimension has two nodes: B1, B2
       Together, four points form a Cartesian grid
The optimization problem: Assign each of the four elements to exactly one grid point
(no more, no less, in a one-to-one mapping)[3].

Why "Cartesian Product" is Special: The set {(a₁,b₁), (a₁,b₂), (a₂,b₁), (a₂,b₂)} is designed as a
"north star" or guide. Its notation intuitively shows you how to use it to fill the grid
efficiently[3].
But any other four-element set could work equally well, if properly used. The magic is not in
the elements themselves but in the functions (projection functions) that assign each
element to its grid position[3].

Relational Reality, Not Intrinsic Properties
Key Insight: There is nothing inherently "Cartesian product-ish" about a set in isolation. The
property of "being a Cartesian product" emerges only through relationships[3].

Specifically:
       The set A × B must have projection functions f and g
       f maps elements down to their position on the A-dimension
       g maps elements over to their position on the B-dimension
       These functions must preserve a unique isomorphism showing that the set fills the
       grid in the optimal way[3]
Insight: The Cartesian product is relationally real, not intrinsically real. It exists in the
system of relationships, not in any single object[3].

Four Revolutionary Properties of Mathematical Objects
Lyons identifies that mathematical definitions exhibit properties we normally associate
with agents or economic systems:
1. Goal-Based
Mathematical objects are defined with respect to achieving goals (filling a grid, counting
efficiently, solving problems). The Cartesian product is the peak of a preference order over
all possible ways to fill a grid[3].

2. Multicausal
No single part of the system produces the property. You cannot isolate "Cartesian product-
ness":
      Not in the set itself
      Not in A
      Not in B
      Not in the functions
      All of the above together, assembled in the right configuration[3]
This echoes Levin's insight that patterns ingressingininto biological systems are
multicausal: they cannot be attributed to genes alone or environment alone but emerge
through the whole system.

3. Polycomputable
The same set can be used to answer different mathematical questions simultaneously and
give different answers. A set can be the Cartesian product under one description and not be
the Cartesian product under another[3].

4. Historical (Non-Temporal)
This is the deepest property. While the Cartesian product exists logically (not temporally), its
definition requires the full system of relationships that make it what it is[3].

Compare to the natural numbers: the number 50 is defined not by the symbol "50" but by its
entire system of relationships—50 is what comes after 49, 48, 47... all the way down to 1, and
before 51, 52, 53... extending infinitely. All those relationships are the "history" that
makes 50 exactly 50 [3].
Similarly, the Cartesian product in historical context is the peak of a preference order:
      Inefficient uses (3-element sets, 5-element sets, misused 4-element sets) occupy the
      bottom tier
      Efficient uses (all 4-element sets properly mapped to the grid) occupy the middle tier
      The canonical A × B occupies the apex[3]

This preference hierarchy is history—not history in time but the full system of
relational comparisons that define the object[3].

Normativity Built Into Mathematics
Radical Implication: Normativity (values, preferences, "should") is not added to
mathematics from outside. It is intrinsic to mathematical structure[3].
Consider the definition of Cartesian product:
      It is a goal (fill the grid efficiently)
      It involves preference (this way is better than that way)
      It is a normative claim (this is what's optimal)
All of this is baked into the mathematical definition itself. Mathematics is not amoral; it is
fundamentally evaluative[3].

Mathematics as Economic Agent
If mathematical objects have goals, preferences, optimization problems, and relational
causation, then mathematics exhibits the structure of economic agency[3].

Lyons draws parallels:
      Price systems in human economics coordinate agents through signals
      Bioelectric communication systems between cells (studied by Levin) coordinate
      through electrochemical signals
      Mathematical relationships coordinate through structural optimization[3]
In each case, we have:

      A system of agents (price-makers, cells, mathematical objects)
      Goals and preferences
      Signals and relationships
      Emergent coordination without top-down control
The implication: Platonic Space is not just a realm of abstract forms. It may be an
economic system—a structured space of resources, constraints, and optimization
problems through which patterns ingressinto the physical world[3].

Functions as Cognitive Glue
Lyons proposes that functions serve as cognitive glue binding mathematical objects into
larger systems—much like bioelectric communication serves as cognitive glue in biological
systems (Levin's work) and price signals serve as cognitive glue in economic systems[3].

Functions do two things:
    1. Control morphology - They give shape and structure to otherwise undifferentiated
       objects. The projection functions f and g give the four elements their grid positions.
    2. Scale goals - They imbue objects with purposes they wouldn't have in isolation. A
       four-element set, alone, has no goal. But through functions, it acquires the goal of
       efficiently filling a grid[3].
This mirrors how:

      Bioelectric interfaces give physical tissues their behavioral goals
      Market mechanisms give individual agents coordinated purposes
      Platonic Space patterns give biological systems morphological and cognitive targets
Conclusion: If functions are cognitive glue in mathematics, and cognitive glue is what
creates agency and goal-orientation, then relationships between mathematical objects
are what make mathematics alive[3].
The Historical Construction of Normativity
Lyons synthesizes his insights into a comprehensive view: normative properties (values,
goals, preferences) are constructed through historical processes[3].
In science and biology, this happens temporally:
      Evolution builds preferences and goals into organisms over time
      Development constructs goal-directedness through temporal processes
      Learning creates new values through experience

In mathematics (where time does not apply), this happens structurally:
      A mathematical object's "history" is the full system of relationships defining it
      Preference orders emerge from comparing solutions to optimization problems
      Normativity is encoded in which solutions are more efficient, elegant, powerful[3]
Both are construction processes. Neither involves discovering pre-existing, intrinsic
values. Instead, values emerge through the dynamics of the system itself—temporal in
biology, relational in mathematics[3].

Implications for Understanding Platonic Space
Lyons' economic analysis deepens Levin's framework:
    1. Platonic Space is structured, not random - The patterns that ingressinto the
       physical world are not arbitrary but follow the logic of optimization and
       preference[3].
    2. Goals and normativity are fundamental - The patterns that make biological forms
       and behaviors are inherently goal-directed, not mechanical or purposeless[3].
    3. Causality flows through relationships - Just as mathematical properties emerge
       from relations between objects, biological and conscious properties emerge from the
       relationships within systems[3].
    4. Dualism is natural - If mathematics (non-physical) causally governs physics
       (physical), and if agents exploit this mathematical causality through goal-seeking
       behaviors, then the non-physical realm's causal efficacy is not mysterious—it is the
       foundation of order[3].

VII. Alexey Tolchinsky: Platonic Space in Psychotherapy
and the Explanatory Gap
The Problem: From Narrative Fallacy to Explanatory Gaps
Dr. Alexey Tolchinsky, a clinical psychologist and neuropsychoanalyst, brings the Platonic
Space framework directly into therapeutic practice, addressing a fundamental problem in
psychology and psychiatry: the explanatory gap between brain and consciousness[4].
Tolchinsky identifies a critical issue in clinical work: narrative fallacy—the tendency of
therapists to construct compelling but misleading causal narratives about their patients'
psychological problems[4].

The Narrative Fallacy Problem
In psychodynamic therapy, therapists construct narratives like:
      "Your childhood trauma caused your depression"
      "Your need for idealization comes from a narcissistic injury in early development"
      "Your splitting defense mechanism explains your relationship patterns"[4]

Each narrative tells a coherent story. Each seems to explain the symptoms. But here's the
problem: the human mind excels at storytelling, not at discovering truth. We naturally
construct false narratives that feel explanatory[4].
The Cost: When therapists commit to one dominant narrative (e.g., "I am a Kleinian
analyst," "I practice EMDR," "I use affective neuroscience"), the risk of narrative fallacy
increases dramatically. The therapist's theoretical lens becomes a filter that distorts
perception, causing them to see patterns that confirm their preferred narrative while
missing others[4].

Why Narrative Fallacy Persists
Tolchinsky identifies three mechanisms driving narrative fallacy in clinical practice:
1. Working Memory Limitations
The human brain can only hold 4-7 items in working memory at once. Therapists must
track:
      Patient's current presentation
      Patient's history (childhood, relationships, trauma)
      Current life circumstances
      The therapeutic relationship itself
      The therapist's own countertransference[4]
Result: The mind simplifies by constructing a narrative—a single coherent story that binds
these disparate elements together. This narrative feels explanatory because it reduces
cognitive load, not because it's accurate[4].

2. The Barnum/Forer Effect
Therapists are susceptible to the Forer effect: the tendency to accept vague, general
statements as uniquely true of oneself. A therapist might say, "Your mother wasn't
emotionally available" (true for ~80% of people) and the client nods in recognition, feeling
uniquely understood[4].

This mutual confirmation bias between therapist and client creates the illusion of
explanatory depth. Both are satisfied with the narrative, so neither questions it[4].

3. Theoretical Identification
When therapists deeply identify with a single school ("I am a Jungian," "I am a
psychodynamic therapist"), they filter all observations through that lens. This is not
intrinsically bad—specialization has merit—but it severely constrains the explanatory
space[4].

Tolchinsky's Proposed Solution: Active Inference and Bayesian Reasoning
Rather than abandoning narrative work, Tolchinsky proposes that therapists adopt active
inference and Bayesian reasoning frameworks—ideas from neuroscience and machine
learning that align with Levin's Platonic Space concept[4].

Hypothesis Testing Without Commitment
The key insight: Generate multiple competing hypotheses rather than a single
dominant narrative[4].
In Bayesian reasoning:
      Start with multiple hypotheses (H1, H2, H3...)
      Assign rough prior probabilities based on base rates
      Gather evidence through careful observation
      Update beliefs in light of evidence
      Allow evidence to disconfirm hypotheses, not just confirm them[4]

In practice: A therapist might entertain:
      Hypothesis 1: The patient's depression arises from unresolved trauma
      (psychodynamic)
      Hypothesis 2: The depression involves maladaptive thought patterns (cognitive)
      Hypothesis 3: The depression involves dysregulated emotional systems (affective
      neuroscience)
      Hypothesis 4: The depression reflects a mismatch between the patient's pattern-
      seeking and current life circumstances (Platonic Space approach)[4]
Rather than defending one narrative, the therapist holds multiple possibilities
simultaneously, gathering evidence that supports or challenges each one[4].

Entropy and Uncertainty
Tolchinsky invokes Maximum Entropy principle (studied by both James and Carl
Friston): internal beliefs should have broad entropy; uncertainty should be maximized
rather than prematurely minimized[4].

This means:
      Keep multiple hypotheses alive longer—don't collapse into a single narrative too
      quickly
      Acknowledge uncertainty explicitly—"I don't fully understand what's happening
      with you" is more honest than false certainty
      Remain open to disconfirming evidence—look for data that challenges your
      preferred hypothesis[4]
The classical psychoanalytic principle of negative capability (Keats/Bion) aligns perfectly:
the therapist sits with uncertainty without rushing to interpretation or closure[4].

Patterns in Psychotherapy: Where Platonic Space Meets Clinical Work
Tolchinsky makes a radical proposal: therapeutic change involves the ingression of new
patterns from Platonic Space[4].

The Explanatory Gap in Therapy
The classical explanatory gap asks: How does talking about the past change neural wiring in
the present?

Standard neuroscience struggles with this:
      Talking is just sound waves
      Those sound waves trigger neural activity
      Neural activity supposedly causes behavioral change
      But the causal mechanism remains mysterious[4]
Platonic Space solution: The talking is not the direct cause. Rather, talking is an interface
—a channel through which new patterns from Platonic Space can ingress into the client's
brain and behavior[4].

Therapeutic Work as Pattern Actualization
When therapy "works," the client reports things like:

      "I see myself differently now"
      "My relationship patterns are changing"
      "I feel more at peace with my past"
      "I have access to capacities I didn't know I had"[4]
These are descriptions of new patterns actualizing in the client's life. The patterns were
not created by the therapist's talk; they were accessed or "activated" through the
therapeutic interface[4].

This parallels Levin's xenobots: the xenobots display unexpected behaviors and gene
expressions that cannot be explained by their genetics alone. Similarly, therapy clients
display unexpected capacities and insights that cannot be explained by their neural wiring
alone[4].
The mechanism: The therapeutic relationship itself becomes a cognitive interface—a
space where the client can interface with patterns from Platonic Space. The therapist's role
is not to install new behaviors but to create conditions for new patterns to
ingressthrough the client's system[4].

Patterns as Self-Organization
Tolchinsky emphasizes that patterns are not imposed from outside but emerge through
self-organization of the client's entire system[4].
In therapy, this looks like:

      The client begins to notice recurring themes in their life story (Levin: "recognizing
      patterns")
      The client develops new ways of understanding themselves (pattern recognition
      across multiple domains)
      The client's physiology, emotion, cognition, and behavior begin coordinating around
      new attractors[4]
All of this happens through the therapeutic relationship but not because of it. The
relationship is the interface; the actual transformation is the actualization of dormant
patterns.

Tolchinsky's Integration with Levin and Lyons
Tolchinsky's clinical work demonstrates the practical application of Levin's Platonic
Space theory and extends Lyons' economic framework:

    1. From Levin: Therapy involves pattern ingression through an interface (the
       therapeutic relationship). The patterns guide reorganization of the client's behavior,
       emotion, and cognition[4].
    2. From Lyons: The patterns that ingress are goal-directed and evaluative. Therapy
       creates a space where the client can reorient toward new goals and values, newly
       actualizing from Platonic Space[4].
    3. Clinical Implication: Rather than imposing a single narrative framework, therapists
       should:
             Hold multiple competing hypotheses
             Recognize that transformation comes through pattern ingression, not
             narrative installation
             Create conditions (through presence, non-judgment, coherent attunement) for
             new patterns to actualize
             Support the client's self-organization rather than directing it[4]
Case Applications: Patterns Beyond Single Narratives
Tolchinsky discusses how the Platonic Space framework resolves classical explanatory
gaps in psychotherapy[4]:

The Mind-Body Problem in Therapy
Classical problem: How does changing a thought or understanding cause changes in the
body (anxiety reduction, mood improvement, pain relief)?
Narrative explanation: The thought causes the emotion causes the body state. This
assumes unidirectional causality[4].
Platonic Space explanation: The thought is an interface through which a coordinating
pattern can actualize. When the client has a new understanding, they are actualizing a
pattern that coordinates mind, emotion, and body simultaneously. It's not a sequence of
causation; it's a unified pattern expressing itself across multiple scales[4].

The Binding Problem in Therapy
Classical problem: How do disparate pieces of experience (visual, auditory, somatic,
emotional, cognitive) come together into a unified sense of self?

Narrative explanation: The narrative binds them—it provides a coherent story that ties
everything together[4].
Platonic Space explanation: A pattern from Platonic Space binds them. The pattern is
goal-directed and coherent at a scale above any individual brain part. The client's
experience unifies around actualizing this pattern[4].

The Hard Problem of Consciousness in Therapy
Classical problem: Why does the client's experience change when their brain function
changes through talk therapy? Why does consciousness transform?
Narrative explanation: Better understanding causes better consciousness (confused
thinking suppressed, clear thinking emerged)[4].

Platonic Space explanation: Consciousness is the view from Platonic Space outward
through the client's interface (their embodied mind). When new patterns actualize,
consciousness shifts because the client is actualizing a different pattern from Platonic

IX. Michael Levin's Extended Synthesis: "Patterns of Form
and Behavior Beyond Emergence"
In his comprehensive ~75-minute talk presented as the kickoff lecture for the 2025 Platonic
Space Symposium, Michael Levin provides a detailed empirical and philosophical defense
of the Platonic Space framework[5].

From Emergence to Goal-Directed Homeostasis
Levin's Core Critique: Rather than attributing unexpected biological patterns to
"emergence"—which he characterizes as merely "a measure of surprise"—he argues for a
fundamentally different conceptual framework[5].
The difference is critical:

      Emergence view: Systems produce surprising outcomes that cannot be predicted
      from parts. The outcome is essentially random or inevitable given sufficient
      complexity
      Platonic Space view: Systems actively navigate toward specific goal-states or
      attractors, guided by patterns that exist as non-physical targets[5]
Evidence from Anatomical Homeostasis:

Levin demonstrates this through regeneration in organisms like axolotl salamanders[5]:
      When a limb is amputated, cells immediately spring into action
      The regenerative process exhibits error correction: cells sense deviation from the
      correct target morphology
      Regeneration terminates precisely when a correct axolotl limb structure has been
      completed
      This is not mechanical rule-following—it is goal-directed homeostasis
Top-Down Causation: Levin presents a striking example of cellular reorientation[5]:

When a tail is grafted onto the flank of a flatworm (Planaria), the tail cells remodel into
limb cells and grow fingers. Why? The cells themselves are functioning normally—there's
no local error signal at the graft site. But there is a global error signal: the body knows a tail
doesn't belong on the flank.
This error signal propagates downward from the global anatomical plan to the
molecular machinery driving cell differentiation. This exemplifies top-down control:
abstract, high-level patterns cascade down to specific molecular events[5].
Levin draws a parallel to human behavior: when you wake with an abstract goal (e.g., "I will
write a book"), that high-level intention must eventually transduce into the precise flow of
potassium and calcium ions across muscle membranes to enact the behavior. This same
hierarchical pattern-implementation occurs in both morphogenesis and cognition[5].

Problem-Solving and Plasticity
Creative Reuse of Tools:

Another striking example from kidney tubule formation in nematodes (C. elegans)[5]:
Normally, 8-10 cells cooperate to form a tubule (lumen) by communicating cell-to-cell.
When experimentally forced to have extra chromosome copies, cells become much larger
but the number stays the same. The system must solve the problem with fewer, larger cells.
The remarkable finding: The cells employ an entirely different mechanism—cytoskeletal
bending rather than cell-to-cell communication—and achieve the exact same structure.
This is creative problem-solving: the system recognizes an unexpected situation and
redeploys other "tricks" from its molecular repertoire to reach the same goal[5].

This looks like the IQ tests humans take: given a new set of objects, can you creatively solve
a problem in a novel way?
Rewriting the Morphogenetic Set-Point
Bioelectrical Information Storage:
A revolutionary finding: the target forms (set-points) that biological systems aim toward
are not hardwired in genetics—they are stored bioelectrically, in the pattern of ion
gradients across cell membranes[5].
Evidence from Two-Headed Flatworms:

Levin's team modified the bioelectrical "set-point" in Planaria, changing the target
morphology from "one head and one tail" to "two heads." The result[5]:
      Cells happily build two-headed flatworms
      This morphological change persists stably
      When two-headed flatworms are cut, they continuously regenerate two-headed
      offspring
      No genetic modification occurred — the DNA is wild-type
Crossing Species Boundaries:

Even more provocatively, bioelectrical modifications can shift target morphologies across
species lines[5]:
      Normal Planaria have triangular heads
      Bioelectrically modified worms can adopt the flattened heads of other species
      (Palina)
      Some modifications produce entirely novel forms that don't match any known
      species (spiky, cylindrical, hybrid shapes)
This demonstrates that the genetic hardware "is quite happy to visit different attractors in
anatomical morphospace." The DNA is versatile; it's the bioelectrical pattern that directs
which attractor the system actualizes[5].

Novel Competencies Unexplained by Evolution
The Xenobots Problem:
Xenobots are synthetic organisms constructed from dissociated cells of frog (Xenopus)
embryos[5]:
      Cells removed from the embryo, placed in a dish, could do many things: die, form a
      flat monolayer, disperse
      Instead, they self-assemble into novel living structures
      These structures swim using ciliary motion, exhibit novel behaviors, and express
      hundreds of genes differently than they would in the embryo[5]

The Critical Point: There has never been evolutionary selection for "being a good
xenobot." No history of xenobot predators, no environmental pressures favoring xenobot
morphology.
Yet xenobots display[5]:
      Kinematic self-replication: They gather loose epithelial cells into piles, which
      mature into next-generation xenobots
      Novel gene expression: Genes for perceiving sound and mechanical stimuli are
      upregulated
      Novel behaviors: When exposed to specific sound frequencies, xenobots change
      behavior stereotypically, then return to baseline when sound stops
      Unexpected competencies: Navigation, collective action, and environmental
      responsivity
How is this possible? Levin's answer: The hardware (frog genome) is not specialized for
frogs alone. It is an interface to Platonic Space—it can actualize many different patterns,
not just those that were ever selected for[5].

Anthropots: Adult Human Cells
To prove this isn't unique to amphibian embryos, Levin's team created anthropots from
adult human tracheal epithelial cells (lung cells)[5]:

      No genetic modification, no synthetic circuits, no scaffolds
      Just cells in a dish, allowed to self-organize
      They form mobile, self-directed organisms with visible cilia
      They display four distinct behavioral states with transitions between them
      They express 9,000+ genes differently than they would in the human trachea[5]
Most strikingly: Anthropots can heal neural tissue damage.
When anthropots are placed on a field of neural cells with a wound scratched through it,
they gather into "superbot clusters," then work over four days to knit the tissue across the
gap. This tissue-repair capacity was never present in isolated lung cells in the body[5].

The implications are profound: Adult human cells, with a completely wild-type genome
(100% human DNA), can actualize patterns from Platonic Space that bear no relationship
to human evolutionary history or embryonic development[5].

The Metric of Morphospace
What is Platonic Space like? Levin identifies key research questions[5]:

    3. What is the chemistry of change? Are patterns dynamic and evolving, not frozen

             Delayed gratification: When obstacles block the algorithm's normal path, it

             Clustering behavior: Instances of the algorithm recognize others running the

             Problem-solving: The algorithm navigates obstacles in ways not explicitly

None of these behaviors are in the algorithm. Yet they emerge reliably, suggesting that
even deterministic systems interface with Platonic patterns.

This implies: Complexity is not the prerequisite for pattern ingression. Even extremely

The Symmetry Between Body and Mind
Levin reiterates a profound insight: There is a fundamental symmetry between
anatomical forms (body morphology) and behavioral forms (kinds of minds)[5].
Both are navigations of pattern-spaces:
      Evolution navigates anatomical morphospace toward body forms

This wasn't incidental to Turing's work on morphogenesis—it was central. Turing

Free Lunches in Biology
The "Free Lunch" Phenomenon:
Biology doesn't pay the full computational cost for many of its capabilities. Instead, it
exploits mathematical properties as "free gifts"[5]:

      Geometry example: When evolving a triangle with three specific angles, evolution
      must work to get two angles right. The third angle is determined for free by the
      mathematical property that angles in a triangle sum to 180°. This saves
      computational effort.
      Logic gates: A voltage-gated ion channel is essentially a transistor. Once you have a
      few transistors, logical properties like NAND gates emerge for free. You don't need to
      evolve the truth table—it's a free gift from mathematics[5].
      Organ formation: Various organs exploit pre-existing mathematical relationships
      between growth rates, diffusion, and symmetry. These relationships don't need to be
      evolved; they're accessed freely from Platonic Space[5]
This concept of "free lunch" is central to understanding why evolution can achieve such
remarkable complexity with relatively modest mutation rates: it's not inventing
everything from scratch; it's gaining access to pre-existing patterns[5].
Physicalism is Insufficient
Levin's Argument:
If the set-points, goals, and patterns that guide morphogenesis are not contained in the DNA

    2. Map the pointers to patterns: Understand which physical interface configurations

    4. Explore the space structure: Determine whether patterns are organized, related, or

    5. Test predictions: Make testable predictions about what patterns will ingress through

XI. Akarsh Kumar: Towards Platonic Intelligence with
Unified Factored Representations
While Michael Levin approaches Platonic Space from biology and consciousness, computer
scientist Akarsh Kumar tackles the problem from AI and machine learning, asking: How
do we design intelligent agents with internal representations that actually
"understand" the world's structure rather than merely mimicking outputs
statistically?[6]

The Core Problem: Fractured vs. Factored Representations
Kumar's Central Claim: Conventional deep learning with stochastic gradient descent
(SGD) produces fractured and entangled neural representations—networks that
achieve perfect behavioral output while their internal organization ignores the world's
fundamental regularities[6].

The World Possesses Deep Structure from Platonic Space
Kumar begins with a foundational observation: Reality is not random. It exhibits[6]:
      Symmetries: Translation invariance (objects behave similarly whether left or right),
      rotation invariance, bilateral symmetry in biological structures
      Self-similarity: Patterns repeat across multiple scales
      Persistence: Objects maintain identity across time
      Shared patterns: Common regularities appear across diverse objects and domains

These features suggest that the world is constrained by a Platonic Space of Forms—a
structured realm of patterns that physical entities instantiate[6].
The Intelligence Problem: For an agent to truly understand the world and control it
effectively, the agent's internal representations must capture these structural
regularities. Output behavior alone is insufficient; the internal organization matters[6].
Current AI Architecture: Partial Solutions and Deep Limitations
Inductive Biases and Their Limits
Modern deep learning attempts to "bake in" known symmetries through architecture
design[6]:

      CNNs encode translation invariance through convolution (objects processed
      identically whether at position X or X+100)
      Transformers encode permutation invariance (set order doesn't matter) through
      attention mechanisms
      The field of symmetry learning or geometric deep learning systematically
      incorporates known regularities
The Problem: These approaches only capture regularities we already know about. What
about the vast space of unknown regularities (e.g., lighting invariance, semantic
relationships)? The solution defaulted to—simply train on massive amounts of data with
SGD and hope the network learns the patterns.

The Paradox: Perfect Behavior, Messy Internals
Despite impressive performance by modern LLMs (ChatGPT, self-driving cars), Kumar
presents evidence that these systems suffer from internally fractured and entangled
representations[6]:
Example 1: Entanglement in GPT-3 Arithmetic

      When asked "I have 3 pencils, 2 pens, 4 erasers. How many things?" → Correct: 9
      Same question reworded: "I have 3 chickens, 2 ducks, 4 geese. How many things?" →
      Incorrect: 10 (not 9)
Explanation: The neural circuitry computing quantity is entangled with object-type
recognition. The network's internal representation doesn't modularly separate
"countability" from "item identity." This is evidence of fractured representation[6].
Example 2: Brittleness to Regularities

Research shows[6]:
      Small changes to numbers in math problems cause performance drops
      Shifting from base-10 to base-9 arithmetic causes dramatic failures
      Networks use "bags of heuristics" rather than principled arithmetic (e.g.,
      approximating 36 + 59 by thinking "36 ≈ 30, 59 ≈ 50, so ≈ 92" rather than actual
      addition)
      "Jagged intelligence": Can solve IMO gold-medal problems but cannot reliably book
      airline tickets
Example 3: Counterfactual Brittleness

When LLMs trained on internet data are evaluated on counterfactual worlds (e.g., code
with 1-indexing instead of 0-indexing), performance plummets. This indicates the models
learned patterns specific to our world rather than underlying regularities[6].
The Fundamental Issue: These networks achieve superhuman output on complex tasks
while their internal organizations remain "spaghetti"—a tangled mess of redundant, non-
modular neurons that don't respect the symmetries and structure of what they're
representing[6].

The CPN Toy Domain: Visualizing Internal Structure
To investigate this problem experimentally, Kumar uses Compositional Pattern Producing
Networks (CPNs) as a visual analogy for understanding neural representations[6].

How CPNs Work
A CPN implicitly represents an image by[6]:
    1. Input: (x, y) coordinates of a pixel, plus distance d from image center
    2. Processing: Feed these three numbers through a neural network
    3. Output: RGB or HSV value (color) for that pixel
    4. Sweep: Repeat for every pixel to visualize the entire image

Why this works: Rather than storing pixel values directly, the network learns a compact
function that generates the image. This allows us to visualize what each neuron in the
network is "doing" by seeing when and where it activates across the image[6].

CPNs as Analogy for LLMs
The brilliance of Kumar's approach: A CPN's visual output is analogous to an LLM's entire
output behavior across all possible prompts. The CPN's internal neuron activations are
analogous to an LLM's internal representation of how it "sees the world"[6].
Critical insight: Two CPNs can produce identical output images yet have qualitatively
different internal encodings. Similarly, two LLMs may produce indistinguishable outputs
while internally representing the world in fundamentally different ways[6].

Pigreeder: Open-Ended Evolution and Emergent Structure
Before comparing SGD-trained networks to Pigreeder-evolved networks, Kumar introduces
Pigreeder—a citizen science experiment where humans interactively bred CPN images[6].

The Pigreeder System
Setup: Humans visited a website to evolve CPN images through iterative selection[6]:

      Displayed three randomly mutated CPN variants
      Humans picked two or three they liked
      Selected images were mutated again
      Iterated indefinitely with no predefined goal
Key Difference from SGD: Unlike supervised learning (optimize toward a target),
Pigreeder involved continuous, shifting human aesthetic preferences guiding an open-
ended evolutionary process[6].
Surprising Result: Structure Without Specification
Expected outcome: Random, meaningless images (what would you expect with no
objective?)
Actual outcome: Humans discovered and created[6]:
      Complex, beautifully structured images
      Objects recognizable from our world (skulls, butterflies, spiderwebs, gears, eyes,
      feathers)
      Images that look organic and intentional despite no explicit instruction

How? The evolutionary process, combined with human preference, created a serendipitous
discovery mechanism. This is not just randomness—it's structured search operating over a
space of possibilities[6].

Three Properties of Pigreeder Evolution
1. Open-Endedness
Like biological evolution, Pigreeder generated endless diversity without a final state. There
is an evolutionary tree where images diverge and branch infinitely, with no predetermined
endpoint[6].
2. Serendipitous Adaptation (Exaptation)

Features evolved for one purpose get repurposed for another[6]:
      Feathers evolved for insulation → later used for flight
      In Pigreeder: Someone evolved an "egg-in-a-hat" image; another user took that
      machinery and adapted it to create a teapot
      Every discovered Pigreeder image displays this stepping-stone property—the
      machinery to create it came from repurposing prior designs
This demonstrates that valuable discoveries emerge not from planning, but from
serendipitous reuse of prior solutions.

3. Evolvability and Emergent Modularity
Biological evolution discovered that encoding symmetries speeds up search[6]:
      If a mutation makes the right hand longer, the left hand should also get longer
      (bilateral symmetry)
      Encoding this axis of variation in the genome saves evolutionary effort—you don't
      waste mutations on asymmetric hand growth
      Similarly, organs are modular; developmental pathways build reusable components

Pigreeder shows the same dynamic: Some images produce high-fitness descendants
(humans keep selecting their variants) because they have good evolvability—their
structure allows small mutations to yield semantically meaningful variations. Non-
evolvable images die out[6].
Pigreeder vs. SGD: The Representation Comparison
Now Kumar compares the internal structure of images evolved via Pigreeder versus trained
via conventional SGD[6].

The Experiment

    2. Extract all pixel data: (x, y, distance) → RGB values
    3. Train two networks on this data:
             Pigreeder pathway: Use the original NEAT-evolved network (evolutionary
             search)
             SGD pathway: Train a conventional MLP (layerized version of NEAT) using

    4. Both achieve perfect output—they reproduce the skull image identically
    5. But examine their internals...

The Results: Stark Contrast
Pigreeder-Evolved Representation (Unified and Factored)[6]:

      Clear function: Individual neurons correspond to meaningful aspects:
            Neuron X: Controls mouth opening
            Neuron Y: Controls left eye winking

      Organized structure: Clear flow from input → abstract features → output

    1. Develop open-ended search algorithms (beyond evolutionary computation) that
       yield factored representations
    2. Measure representation quality directly (Kumar's weight-sweep visualizations
       could be extended to LLMs via mechanistic interpretability)
    3. Test serendipitous curricula on diverse tasks; compare to standard SGD
       curriculum
    4. Optimize for adaptability: Train agents for robustness to environmental
       perturbations; measure resulting internal structure
    5. Scale open-ended search: Pigreeder showed human-driven evolution; can we
       automate/scale this process?

Conclusion: From Scaling Alone to Structure-First Intelligence
Kumar's work suggests a fundamental alternative to the current AI paradigm[6]:

      Current dogma: Scale networks and data; hope structure emerges
      Kumar's proposal: Design learning processes to respect and build structure;
      emergence becomes a side-effect, not the goal
This aligns perfectly with Levin's Platonic Space framework: Rather than brute-force
pattern-matching, intelligent systems should be interfaces to Platonic patterns—internal
organizations that reflect the actual structure of the world[6].
The implications extend beyond AI: If human intelligence also operates via structure-
respecting representations, then human learning (education, skill development, creativity)
might be optimized not by more content, but by better structure-discovery curricula—
learning in serendipitous sequences that build deeper understanding[6].

X. Conclusion: From Ancient Forms to Empirical Science
The history of Platonic Space spans 2,400 years: from Plato's metaphysical intuition that
reality is structured by non-physical Forms, through centuries of philosophical refinement,
to contemporary philosophy of mathematics, and now to Michael Levin's empirical
research program in biology and consciousness, supported by complementary
perspectives from economics (Benjamin Lyons), clinical psychology (Alexey Tolchinsky),
and AI/machine learning (Akarsh Kumar).

What was once purely speculative has become investigable. The notion that non-physical
patterns guide biological form and behavior, once relegated to mysticism or metaphysics,
can now be subjected to rigorous experimental scrutiny through novel interfaces, synthetic
organisms, computational measurement, and machine learning experiments[2][5][6].
Whether Platonic Space is ultimately vindicated or refuted remains to be seen. But in
transforming an ancient philosophical intuition into a testable research agenda pursued by
researchers across multiple disciplines, the Platonic Space symposium demonstrates how
philosophy and empirical science can converge to illuminate the deepest questions about
the nature of mind, matter, meaning, and existence.
