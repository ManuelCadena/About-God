**summary**

The lecture explores the potential connection between consciousness and quantum mechanics, a speculative idea not widely accepted in mainstream neuroscience. David Eagleman introduces the fundamental question of why subjective experience arises from the brain\'s biological and electrochemical processes. Roger Penrose, a mathematical physicist, argues that consciousness cannot be explained by algorithms, pointing to Gödel\'s incompleteness theorem as evidence that human understanding transcends computation. Penrose suggests that the non-computable nature of consciousness might require a different kind of physics, specifically the collapse of the quantum wave function. He felt that the collapse of the wave function had to be a major part of the physics which is responsible for evoking consciousness. Stuart Hammeroff, an anesthesiologist, proposes that microtubules within brain cells could be the site of quantum processes relevant to consciousness. Hammeroff suggests that these microtubules, tiny cylindrical structures inside neurons, might orchestrate quantum processes and that the collapse of quantum states within them could correspond to moments of conscious experience. Hammeroff also addresses the \"hard problem\" of consciousness, questioning why physical processes in the brain give rise to subjective experience. The orchestrated objective reduction (Orch OR) theory, developed by Penrose and Hammeroff, posits that consciousness arises from orchestrated quantum computations in microtubules, specifically during the collapse of the wave function. Hammeroff suggests that entanglement across different brain regions, facilitated by microtubules, could solve the binding problem, explaining how distinct features processed in different brain regions integrate into a seamless perception. Penrose and Hammeroff argue that their theory blurs the line between physics and philosophy, suggesting that consciousness may be a fundamental property of the universe, not just a feature of brains. Penrose believes that current AI systems are not conscious and won\'t be with more elements, and that a quantum computer might be conscious if designed with the right architecture. Hammeroff believes that AI consciousness is not inevitable, emphasizing the difference between silicon and organic carbon, with the latter having quantum properties. Eagleman concludes by emphasizing the importance of exploring speculative ideas while remaining grounded in empirical evidence, encouraging young scientists to pursue big, risky questions about consciousness.
