Set Theory as Foundation of Mathematics 
Historical Development and Cantor's Contributions 
Set theory emerged as the mathematical study of infinity and has historically served as the foundation for all mathematics, with the appealing property that all mathematical concepts can be formalized within set theory ‎‎ 00:01:39
. The field originated with Cantor's work on cardinality, introducing the notation A ≤ B to indicate that set A has size not greater than set B through the existence of a one-to-one function ‎‎ 00:02:32

Cantor's Theorem and Different Infinities 
Cantor's fundamental theorem demonstrates that for every set A, A has smaller size than its power set, establishing that infinite sets come in different sizes just as finite sets do ‎‎ 00:03:52
. The diagonal argument proves that natural numbers have smaller cardinality than real numbers, showing the existence of multiple levels of infinity ‎‎ 00:04:58

Axiom of Choice and Cardinal Numbers 
The axiom of choice, equivalent to the assertion that every two sets can be compared in size, extends the intuitive notion of size comparison to infinite sets ‎‎ 00:07:16
. Despite historical controversy due to its non-constructive nature and paradoxical consequences like the Banach-Tarski paradox, it has become widely accepted for its mathematical utility ‎‎ 00:08:14
. Under this axiom, infinities are well-ordered as cardinal numbers denoted by ℵ₀, ℵ₁, etc., where ℵ₀ represents the smallest infinity ‎‎ 00:08:38

The Continuum Hypothesis 
Cantor's continuum hypothesis postulates that no set exists with cardinality between the natural numbers and the power set of natural numbers ‎‎ 00:10:16

Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems 
Historical Context and Hilbert's Program 
The incompleteness theorems emerged from questions about mathematical reliability and the quest to demonstrate that axioms of arithmetic or set theory could settle every question in their respective domains while remaining consistent ‎‎ 00:17:34
. Hilbert's program sought to show that axioms suffice to answer all questions and prove their own consistency ‎‎ 00:17:47

First Incompleteness Theorem 
Gödel's first theorem establishes that any theory T satisfying three conditions - sufficient strength for basic arithmetic, computably enumerable axioms, and consistency - must be incomplete ‎‎ 00:19:30
. The theorem demonstrates that no axiomatic system can answer all mathematical questions, as any enhanced theory remains subject to incompleteness ‎‎ 00:20:20

Second Incompleteness Theorem

Philosophical Responses to Undecidability

Predicativism rejects mathematical objects that cannot be explicitly constructed, distinguishing between definite statements like Pythagoras' theorem and indefinite ones like the continuum hypothesis ‎‎ 00:28:04

Pluralism

Ultra-Platonism (Realism)

New Axiom Candidates

Large Cardinal Axioms
